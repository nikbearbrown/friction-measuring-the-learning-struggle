# PART 1 — BOOK CONCEPT AND THESIS

## Book concept summary

> This book teaches **educators, instructional designers, and educational
> technology decision-makers** that the artifact — the essay, the exam,
> the project — is no longer sufficient evidence of genuine learning,
> and that the behavioral signatures surrounding an artifact's production
> (friction traces) constitute an independent, measurable, and harder-to-game
> evidence stream. It fills the gap left by AI detection tools (which answer
> the wrong question), engagement analytics (which measure the wrong thing),
> and general AI-in-education discourse (which names the problem without
> giving practitioners the measurement framework). It succeeds if the reader
> finishes knowing what friction traces are, why they exist, what each of the
> seven GLP components measures, and how to begin building process-observable
> assessment into their own practice.

**One-sentence logline:**
The artifact used to be proof of the process — now we have to measure
the struggle itself, and this book shows exactly how.

**Central thesis:**
Generative AI has permanently decoupled the artifact from the cognitive
process that used to produce it. The essay, the exam, the clinical note —
each can now be produced without the learning that assessment was designed
to evidence. AI detection cannot solve this because it answers the wrong
question (did a human type this?) rather than the right one (did a human
learn this?). The solution is process-based measurement: seven behavioral
signatures that genuine cognitive engagement leaves in observable data,
which AI cannot produce without triggering the very neurological events
that constitute learning. The GLP framework formalizes these signatures
as a second evidence stream that instructors combine with artifact quality
in whatever proportion their professional judgment warrants.

**Why "Friction Traces" as the organizing concept:**
The word "friction" does two things simultaneously. It names the
productive struggle that genuine learning requires — the difficulty
that is not a bug but the mechanism. And it names the observable
residue that struggle leaves behind in behavioral data. Friction
traces are both the evidence of learning and the cause of it. That
double meaning is the book's central insight.

# PART 2 — LEARNER PROFILE

## Primary reader (one specific person)

A high school science department chair who has watched AI-assisted
submissions multiply since 2023. She can spot the fluency trap in
student writing — the smoothness that doesn't match the student's
in-class performance. She grades papers and suspects AI involvement
but cannot prove it, and she is uncomfortable with accusation-based
processes. She has heard of AI detectors and does not trust them.
She wants a framework that shifts the question from "did you use AI?"
to "did you learn?" She is not a researcher. She is a practitioner
who needs a working methodology.

**Secondary reader:**
Instructional designers and educational technology leads at higher
education institutions who need to explain to faculty why engagement
analytics are insufficient and what process-based measurement adds.

## Prior knowledge assumed

- Active teaching or curriculum role
- Direct experience with AI-assisted submissions
- Basic familiarity with formative assessment concepts
- Awareness that AI detection tools are unreliable

## Prior knowledge NOT assumed

- Research literacy (the Bjork distinction, the Bastani study,
  the neurobiological basis — all introduced from scratch)
- Knowledge of the GLP framework or the seven components
- Understanding of spaced repetition or retrieval practice theory
- Familiarity with Irreducibly Human, AI+1, or Medhavy

## Prior misconceptions

1. "The solution to AI in education is better detection" — the book's
   foundational correction; detection answers the wrong question
2. "Engagement metrics tell me whether students are learning" — the
   Bastani finding corrects this; engagement and learning decouple
   with AI access
3. "If a student's artifact is high quality, they must have learned
   something" — the fluency trap; high-quality output is now available
   without the cognitive process that used to produce it
4. "Friction and struggle are signs something is wrong" — the
   desirable difficulties literature corrects this; productive struggle
   is the mechanism, not the symptom
5. "Process observation is surveillance" — the book distinguishes
   process observation for learning support from process observation
   for surveillance; the distinction is in purpose and data ownership

# PART 4 — FIELD POSITIONING

## The gap this book fills

The AI-in-education discourse is saturated with two arguments:
(1) detect and punish AI use, and (2) accept AI and redesign
assessment. Neither argument gives practitioners a measurement
framework for the question they actually need to answer: did
genuine learning occur?

No existing practitioner-facing book explains the GLP framework,
defines friction traces in accessible language, or gives educators
the tools to begin process-based assessment without a platform.

## Positioning statements

vs. AI detection tools and the literature around them:
"Unlike AI detection approaches, which attempt to determine whether
a human produced the artifact, this book gives educators a framework
for determining whether genuine learning occurred — a question that
is both more important and more answerable."

vs. general AI-in-education books:
"Unlike books that frame AI in education as a policy problem or a
pedagogical philosophy question, this book gives educators seven
specific, measurable behavioral signatures of genuine learning and
explains how to begin observing them."

vs. learning analytics literature:
"Unlike learning analytics approaches that optimize on engagement
metrics, this book focuses on the behavioral signatures that genuine
cognitive engagement leaves behind — signals that engagement analytics
systematically miss."

# PART 5 — THREE-ACT LEARNING ARC

## Arc statement

This book takes the reader from **"I suspect AI was involved but
I can't prove it and I feel uncomfortable with accusation"** to
**"I have a measurement framework that shifts the question from
'did you use AI?' to 'did you learn?', and I know how to begin
applying it"** — by first establishing why the artifact is no
longer sufficient (Act One), then introducing the seven friction
components and the neurobiological basis for each (Act Two), then
giving the practitioner the tools to begin implementing process-based
assessment in their own context (Act Three).

## Act One — The Artifact Is No Longer Enough (Chapters 1–2)

Starting state: Reader knows AI-assisted work is increasing and
existing tools don't solve the problem. Does not have a framework
for why.

Ending state: Reader understands the decoupling problem precisely,
understands why detection cannot solve it, and understands that
the right question is not "did you use AI?" but "did you learn?"

Pebble: Chapter 1 opens with the Bastani finding — same AI, two
wrappers, students who looked engaged and learned nothing. Not a
cheating story. A measurement story.

## Act Two — The Seven Signals (Chapters 3–9)

Starting state: Reader understands the framework needs. Does not
know what the seven components are.

Ending state: Reader understands each component, its neurobiological
basis, its behavioral signature, what genuine learning looks like
on it versus borrowed certainty, and how to begin observing it
without a platform.

Hardest moment: Chapter 3 (Temporal Engagement Pattern) — the
first component requires the reader to accept that time-on-task
is currently being measured wrong. Chapter 9 (Social Knowledge
Texture) — the most subjective component and the most powerful.

## Act Three — Building the Second Evidence Stream (Chapters 10–11)

Starting state: Reader understands all seven components. Needs to
know how to combine them and begin using them.

Ending state: Reader understands the ensemble logic, understands
the instructor-as-meta-model principle, and has a starting point
for implementing process-based assessment in their own practice —
beginning with the two or three components most accessible given
their current infrastructure.

Transfer test: The reader who finishes Chapter 11 can explain
to a colleague why AI detection is the wrong tool, what friction
traces are, and which two components they are going to begin
observing this term.

### CHAPTER 1 — The Artifact Is No Longer Enough

**One-line:** The reader learns that the decoupling of artifact from
process is permanent, why detection cannot solve it, and what the
right question actually is.

**Opening:**
A university deploys GPT-4 as a tutoring assistant. Engagement
climbs. Satisfaction is high. Practice performance is 48% above
the no-AI control. The end-of-term exam arrives. The students
who used the AI most score 17 percentage points below the students
who used no AI at all.

The engagement metrics showed success. The exam showed something else.

This is the Bastani 2025 finding. It is not a cheating story.
It is a measurement story. The platform was measuring the wrong thing.

**Core content blocks:**
1. The causal chain that broke — the original chain (Genuine Learning
   → Cognitive Process → Artifact) and the bypass (AI Generation →
   Artifact); why the artifact now has two causal pathways; why
   this is permanent, not a temporary condition
2. Why detection cannot solve this — three structural limitations:
   temporally bounded (always fighting the last model), wrong question
   (did a human type this vs. did a human learn this), perverse
   incentives (students learn to game the detector, not the material)
3. The right question — not "did you use AI?" but "did you learn?";
   why this question is both more important and more answerable;
   the shift from artifact analysis to process observation
4. What the book will do — introduce seven behavioral signatures
   of genuine learning; explain the neurobiological basis for each;
   give practitioners tools to begin observing them without a platform

**Worked example:**
The Bastani study as a case, walked through in detail. Two conditions:
unguarded GPT-4 and teacher-designed GPT with guardrails. Same model,
same students, same content. Different wrappers. Opposite outcomes
on the unassisted exam. The engagement metrics were identical. The
learning evidence was not.

**Exercises (3):**
1. (Analyze) Name one assessment in your current practice where
   you are confident the artifact is still reliable evidence of
   genuine learning. Name one where you are no longer confident.
   What is different between the two?
2. (Analyze) A student submits an exceptional essay. You suspect
   AI involvement but cannot prove it. Write the question you
   actually want to ask — not "did you use AI?" but a question
   that probes whether genuine learning occurred.
3. (Evaluate) An AI detection tool flags 8% of student submissions.
   Name two ways this figure could be both an undercount and an
   overcount simultaneously.

**Bridge:** Detection answers the wrong question. The right question
requires measuring the process, not the artifact. The process leaves
traces. Chapter 2 explains why.

### CHAPTER 2 — What Genuine Learning Leaves Behind

**One-line:** The reader learns why genuine cognitive engagement
produces behavioral traces that borrowed certainty cannot, and
why those traces are the basis of the seven-component framework.

**Opening:**
Friction traces are not a metaphor. They are behavioral consequences
of neurobiological events that constitute genuine learning. A student
who offloaded their essay to an AI did not trigger the prediction
errors, BDNF expression, or dendritic spine formation that genuine
engagement with difficult material produces. The AI cannot produce
the behavioral traces those events leave, because the events did
not occur.

**Core content blocks:**
1. The neurobiological basis — prediction error and dopamine release;
   BDNF upregulation under moderate cognitive challenge; dendritic
   spine formation; why these events produce behavioral traces;
   why AI bypass prevents these events (accessible, not clinical)
2. The storage-retrieval distinction — Bjork in plain language:
   retrieval strength (how accessible something is right now) versus
   storage strength (how thoroughly it is encoded); why high
   immediate performance does not indicate genuine learning; why
   decay curves are diagnostic
3. The fluency trap — why AI-generated text produces fluency without
   understanding; why high artifact quality may be mild negative
   evidence of genuine engagement; why the rough, searching artifact
   is often more diagnostic than the polished one
4. What friction is — productive struggle as the mechanism, not the
   symptom; the desirable difficulties literature in plain language;
   why difficulty is the thing, not the obstacle to the thing
5. The seven components preview — one sentence per component;
   what each one measures; why the combination is more robust
   than any single trace

**Worked example:**
Two students, same assignment. Student A spent three hours on the
essay, produced three drafts, the final version has places where
she clearly lost the thread and found it again. Student B spent
20 minutes, submitted a polished first draft with no revision history.
The instructor reads both essays. They are comparable in quality.
What does each student's behavior around the essay tell the instructor?

**Exercises (3):**
1. (Understand) In your own learning — something you genuinely
   struggled to understand — describe the behavioral traces your
   struggle left. Now describe what those traces would have looked
   like if you had read a clear AI explanation instead.
2. (Apply) Name one piece of student behavior you have noticed
   that was NOT in the artifact but told you something about
   whether genuine learning had occurred. What friction trace
   does this correspond to?
3. (Understand) Explain the fluency trap to a skeptical colleague
   in two sentences. The explanation must not require them to
   know what AI is.

**Bridge:** Genuine learning leaves seven kinds of behavioral traces.
The next seven chapters cover each one: what it is, why it exists,
what it looks like, and how to begin observing it.

### CHAPTER 3 — Y1: Temporal Engagement Pattern

**One-line:** Genuine engagement distributes time according to
difficulty. Borrowed certainty distributes time according to
output length. The correlation between the two is the signal.

**Opening:**
A student spends 47 minutes on a module. Canvas logs 47 minutes.
What Canvas does not log is whether those 47 minutes were spent
on the three concepts the student found difficult, or distributed
evenly across the module regardless of difficulty — which is what
AI-assisted processing looks like.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What TEP measures — the correlation between item difficulty
   and time-on-task; why genuine processing tracks difficulty;
   why borrowed certainty tracks output length instead
2. The cognitive load basis — intrinsic cognitive load and element
   interactivity; why harder material demands more processing time
   when the student is doing the processing
3. Genuine signature — positive correlation; time bunches on
   difficult concepts; re-reading, backtracking, pause events
4. Borrowed certainty signature — near-zero correlation;
   time tracks explanation length; uniform distribution across
   difficulty levels
5. How to observe without a platform — think-aloud protocols;
   asking students to annotate where they spent time and why;
   draft timestamps if students submit work in stages
6. What a platform adds — clickstream data at sub-paragraph
   granularity; automatic difficulty-time correlation; Y1 score

**Worked example:**
A chemistry instructor asks students to annotate their problem sets:
"Put a checkmark next to problems that felt easy, a question mark
next to ones that felt hard." Compares annotation patterns to
test performance. Students whose question marks track actual
difficulty show the genuine TEP signature. Students whose question
marks are random or absent show borrowed certainty's signature.

### CHAPTER 4 — Y2: Error Trajectory Coherence

**One-line:** Genuine misconceptions produce coherent errors that
evolve predictably as the mental model develops. Random errors
produce no coherent pattern. The coherence is the signal.

**Opening:**
A student makes the same kind of mistake three times in a row,
then makes a different kind of mistake, then gets the next five
right. The instructor notices the pattern. The first three errors
were consistent with a specific misconception. The change in error
type signals the mental model updated. The subsequent correct
answers confirm it.

This is not a lucky accident. It is the reward prediction error
mechanism doing its job.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What ETC measures — whether errors follow conceptually adjacent
   paths through the misconception space; the conceptual adjacency
   matrix in plain language
2. The prediction error basis — each error is a gap between
   expectation and outcome; the dopamine signal drives model update;
   updates are cumulative, so errors follow a path
3. Genuine signature — errors cluster around the current
   misconception; error type changes at the update event;
   later performance reflects the updated model
4. Borrowed certainty signature — errors are random with respect
   to conceptual adjacency; no coherent path; error type does
   not predict subsequent performance
5. How to observe without a platform — coding formative assessment
   errors by misconception type; simple misconception maps;
   looking for error-type changes as update signals
6. What a platform adds — automatic coding against a misconception
   adjacency matrix; Y2 score; early detection of stuck misconceptions

**Worked example:**
An algebra teacher codes her class's errors on a fraction unit
by misconception type: numerator-denominator confusion, operation-
on-numerator-only error, sign error. Maps each student's error
trajectory across five assessments. Students whose trajectory
moves from one misconception type to adjacent types show genuine
ETC. Students whose errors are distributed randomly across types
show borrowed certainty's signature.

### CHAPTER 5 — Y3: Cross-Context Transfer

**One-line:** Genuine understanding transfers to new surface
features. Borrowed certainty pattern-matches to the original
context and fails when surface features change. The transfer
gap is the signal.

**Opening:**
A medical student knows beta blockade. She can define it, explain
the mechanism, recite the indications. Then she encounters a
clinical scenario that does not mention "beta blockade" by name —
it describes a patient presentation and asks for management. She
does not recognize that beta blockade applies. She had the surface
features. She did not have the schema.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What CCT measures — near transfer (same concept, similar
   surface features) vs. far transfer (same concept, different
   surface features); the transfer gap as the diagnostic
2. The schema formation basis — germane cognitive load and
   schema development; why genuine engagement produces abstract
   representations; why surface learning produces context-bound
   representations
3. Genuine signature — near and far transfer both elevated;
   small transfer gap; concept recognized across surface variations
4. Borrowed certainty signature — near transfer high, far transfer
   low; large positive transfer gap; pattern-matching without
   schema
5. How to observe without a platform — designing one near-transfer
   and one far-transfer item for each major concept; the far-transfer
   item deliberately uses different surface features; comparing
   performance on both
6. What a platform adds — automated transfer problem sets;
   Y3 score; concept-level transfer gap tracking

**Worked example:**
A physics teacher designs a near-transfer item on Newton's third
law (hockey puck and stick, standard textbook format) and a
far-transfer item (astronaut pushing off a wall in space, no
force language). Compares student performance on both. Students
whose far-transfer performance is within 10% of near-transfer
show genuine CCT. Students who score well on near and poorly
on far show borrowed certainty's transfer gap.

### CHAPTER 6 — Y4: Uncertainty Calibration

**One-line:** Genuine learners develop accurate models of what
they know and don't know. Borrowed certainty inherits the AI's
confidence without the knowledge that justifies it. The
calibration gap is the signal.

**Opening:**
Two students complete a quiz. Both score 75%. Before the quiz,
Student A predicted she would score around 70%. Student B predicted
she would score 95%. Same performance, different calibration.
Student A knows what she knows. Student B has borrowed the AI's
confidence. When a question is genuinely hard, Student A will
flag it. Student B will not see it as hard.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What UC measures — the gap between expressed confidence before
   an item and actual performance on it; the Brier score as a
   formalization; direction of miscalibration as additional signal
2. The effortful encoding basis — why genuine retrieval practice
   produces calibrated uncertainty; why the student who struggles
   to retrieve learns both the content and the limits of their
   knowledge
3. Genuine signature — confidence tracks actual knowledge; low
   confidence on genuinely hard items; high confidence on genuinely
   easy items; calibration improves over the course
4. Borrowed certainty signature — systematically high confidence
   regardless of item difficulty; calibration does not improve
   over the course; confidence does not predict performance
5. How to observe without a platform — one-question confidence
   elicitation before any assessment: "On a scale of 1-3, how
   confident are you in your answer?" Low cost, high signal
6. What a platform adds — automated calibration tracking;
   Y4 score; calibration trajectory over the term

**Worked example:**
A nursing instructor adds a single confidence question to her
weekly quizzes: "How confident are you that you answered this
correctly? (Not confident / Somewhat confident / Very confident)"
Tracks the gap between confidence and accuracy for each student
over eight weeks. Students whose gap narrows over the term
are developing genuine Y4. Students whose gap stays constant
or widens are showing borrowed certainty's signature.

### CHAPTER 7 — Y5: Social Knowledge Texture

**One-line:** Genuine encounter with material produces specific
confusions, real-time position changes, and productive uncertainty
in discussion. Borrowed certainty produces smooth generic statements.
The texture is the signal.

**Opening:**
An instructor asks a seminar question. One student says: "I think
the mechanism works through X, but I'm confused about why Y doesn't
interfere — can you help me see why?" Another student says:
"The mechanism is a complex interplay of multiple factors working
in conjunction to produce the observed outcomes." Both students
answered. One is thinking. One is performing.

The instructor knows the difference immediately. Y5 formalizes
what the instructor already knows.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What SKT measures — four markers in discussion: personal
   encounter (specific confusion the student can articulate),
   below-surface engagement (connection to prior knowledge that
   emerged during genuine struggle), productive uncertainty
   (questions that arose from real engagement), real-time
   development (position changes during the discussion itself)
2. The genuine contact basis — why authentic encounter with
   material produces texture that cannot be manufactured; why
   the texture of having genuinely struggled is specific in ways
   that borrowed certainty's generic smoothness is not
3. Genuine signature — specific confusions; real-time position
   changes; questions that could only arise from genuine engagement;
   uncertainty that develops during the conversation
4. Borrowed certainty signature — generic statements; no real-time
   development; rehearsed positions; no specific confusions
5. How to observe without a platform — the SKT rubric (four
   markers, scored 0–2 each); discussion coding; office hour
   conversations; one-minute papers asking "what confused you
   most about this material?"
6. What a platform adds — automated analysis of discussion
   transcripts; Y5 score; identification of students showing
   borrowed certainty's signature in written interaction

**Worked example:**
An instructor uses the SKT rubric on five discussion posts from
the same assignment. Two posts score high on all four markers —
specific confusions named, position changes documented, questions
that couldn't come from a summary. Three posts score near zero —
smooth, generic, no texture. The rubric makes explicit what the
instructor already felt reading them.

### CHAPTER 8 — Y6: Retrieval Strength Decay Signature

**One-line:** Genuine learning shows the spacing effect —
performance holds up better after distributed practice than
massed practice. Borrowed certainty shows monotonic decay.
The decay curve shape is the signal.

**Opening:**
Two students score 85% on a quiz immediately after studying.
Three weeks later, Student A scores 78%. Student B scores 42%.
Same immediate performance. Completely different storage strength.
The difference is visible in the decay curve. The quiz score
was not sufficient evidence. The decay signature was.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What RSDS measures — performance at immediate, short delay,
   and long delay assessment; the spacing benefit (improvement
   from distributed vs. massed practice) as the primary diagnostic
2. The storage-retrieval distinction revisited — why retrieval
   strength and storage strength can dissociate; why high immediate
   performance does not predict long-term retention; why the decay
   curve is the more honest measure
3. Genuine signature — slower decay; spacing benefit present;
   performance at long delay better than massed-practice prediction
4. Borrowed certainty signature — monotonic decay; no spacing
   benefit; performance collapses without continued AI access
5. How to observe without a platform — embedding one or two
   items from previous units in each quiz; tracking performance
   on those items over time; the decay curve is visible in
   existing assessment data
6. What a platform adds — FSRS scheduling; Y6 score per concept
   node; automatic identification of concepts with high decay rates

**Worked example:**
A history instructor adds three questions from previous units
to each weekly quiz — not announced in advance. Tracks each
student's performance on these "decay probe" questions over
the semester. Students who maintain performance on decay probes
show genuine Y6. Students whose decay probe performance
collapses by week 6 show borrowed certainty's signature even
if their current-unit quiz scores remain high.

### CHAPTER 9 — Y7: Scaffolding Response Curve

**One-line:** A student with genuine partial understanding responds
to a partial hint almost as well as a full hint. A student with
borrowed certainty requires the full answer. The ratio is the signal.

**Opening:**
A student is stuck on a problem. The instructor offers a partial
hint — one piece of the structure, not the solution. Genuine partial
understanding activates: the student uses the hint to connect
what they already know to what they were missing. Borrowed certainty
has nothing to activate: the student needs the full answer because
there is no developing model for the hint to engage.

The Zone of Proximal Development is a structural property of a
genuinely developing mental model. It is directly observable in
hint response patterns.

**Core content blocks:**
1. What SRC measures — the performance gain from a partial hint
   relative to the performance gain from a full hint; Y7 as the
   ratio; the integration measure (does the student need the hint
   again next problem or did they integrate it?)
2. The ZPD basis — Vygotsky in plain language; why genuine partial
   understanding creates a region where targeted support activates
   underlying structure; why borrowed certainty has no such region
3. Genuine signature — high Y7 ratio; partial hint nearly as useful
   as full hint; positive integration (hint not needed again)
4. Borrowed certainty signature — low Y7 ratio; only full hint
   produces improvement; no integration (hint needed again)
5. How to observe without a platform — structured hint experiments
   in office hours or tutoring; first offer the concept framing
   (partial), then offer the worked approach (full); note which
   students improve after partial vs. needing full
6. What a platform adds — automated hint-level tracking; Y7 score;
   early identification of students whose ZPD has not yet formed
   for a concept

**Worked example:**
A calculus tutor works with five students who are stuck on
related rates problems. For each student, she offers: first,
a partial hint (identify which quantities are changing and
how they relate). Then, if needed, a full hint (here is the
equation structure). She notes which students improve after
the partial hint alone and which require the full. The partial-
hint-responders are showing genuine Y7. The full-hint-requirers
are showing borrowed certainty's signature — no ZPD to activate.

## ACT THREE — BUILDING THE SECOND EVIDENCE STREAM (Chapters 10–11)

---

### CHAPTER 10 — The Ensemble: Why Seven and Not One

**One-line:** The combination of seven partially independent signals
is substantially more robust to gaming than any individual signal —
manufacturing all seven simultaneously approaches the cost of
genuine engagement.

**Opening:**
A student who wants to game a single signal can. Spend more time
on hard questions (fake Y1). Make coherent errors on purpose (fake
Y2). Say "I'm confused about X" in discussion (fake Y5). But
gaming all seven simultaneously requires knowing which concepts
are adjacent (Y2), what genuine transfer looks like for each
concept (Y3), what the actual difficulty distribution is (Y1),
what your actual performance level is (Y4), how to produce
textured discussion that tracks genuine conceptual development
(Y5), when to show decay and when not to (Y6), and what partial
understanding would look like for each concept (Y7).

At that point, the gaming has become indistinguishable from learning.

**Core content blocks:**
1. The ensemble logic — why different statistical structures
   require different models; why different failure modes make
   combination more robust; the three-layer architecture in
   plain language (component models, tier-conditioned combination,
   meta-model with credible interval)
2. The gaming cost argument — manufacturing all seven traces
   approaches the cognitive cost of genuine engagement; this is
   not a claim the framework is gaming-proof, it is a claim
   the framework is gaming-expensive
3. Tier calibration — why the same component is more or less
   diagnostic at different cognitive tiers; why Y5 is the
   primary signal at the social cognition tier and Y3 is
   primary at the causal reasoning tier; the Irreducibly Human
   taxonomy as the calibration framework
4. The instructor as meta-model — the GLP score is not a grade;
   the instructor combines GLP score with artifact quality
   in whatever proportion professional judgment warrants;
   high-stakes summative assessment late in a course weights
   artifact heavily; early formative assessment weights GLP heavily
5. What the framework is NOT claiming — artifacts are not
   worthless; process observation is not always more informative
   than artifact observation; the claim is that process adds
   independent information, not that it adds more

**Worked example:**
Two students, six weeks of data, all seven components visible.
Student A shows genuine signatures on five components, mixed
on two. Student B shows borrowed certainty signatures on four,
genuine on three. The instructor uses the GLP profile alongside
the artifact quality score to form an assessment judgment.
The profile doesn't make the decision. It gives the instructor
evidence she didn't have before.

**Exercises (3):**
1. (Analyze) For each of the seven components, name the one
   most important reason it is difficult to fake — the specific
   knowledge a student would need to produce a convincing
   genuine signature without actually learning.
2. (Analyze) A student's GLP profile shows genuine Y1, Y4,
   and Y6 but borrowed certainty on Y2, Y3, and Y5. What
   does this profile suggest about the nature of their
   engagement? What would you ask them?
3. (Evaluate) Your institution wants to use GLP scores in
   final grade calculations. Name two conditions that would
   need to be true for this to be appropriate, and one
   condition that would make it inappropriate.

**Bridge:** The framework is built. Chapter 11 gives the
practitioner a starting point for their own context.

### CHAPTER 11 — Building Your Second Evidence Stream

**One-line:** The reader identifies the two or three GLP components
most accessible in their current practice and specifies a first
implementation.

**Opening:**
You do not need a platform to begin. You need to pick two
components, add them to what you already do, and observe
what you find. The full seven-component framework is the
destination. One component is the starting point.

**Core content blocks:**
1. The triage framework — which components are most accessible
   given current infrastructure; the component accessibility
   ladder from easiest (Y4 confidence elicitation, minimal LMS
   modification) to most resource-intensive (Y5 SKT coding,
   requires trained raters)
2. The starting point for different contexts:
   - K-12: Y4 (confidence question on any quiz) and Y5
     (one-minute paper on what confused you)
   - Higher education: Y6 (decay probes in existing quizzes)
     and Y3 (one near-transfer and one far-transfer item per
     major concept)
   - Clinical education: Y7 (structured hint experiment in
     case discussion) and Y5 (SKT rubric on case presentations)
3. What the first term looks like — collecting two components,
   not drawing conclusions too quickly, using the data to
   have different conversations with students, not to make
   different grade calculations
4. The institutional design implication — longitudinal process
   documentation as primary rather than supplementary evidence;
   what it would mean to redesign assessment infrastructure
   around process observation; the urgency argument
5. The ethics of process observation — the distinction between
   observation for learning support and observation for
   surveillance; who controls the data; what it is used for

**Worked example:**
A community college instructor in her first semester of
process-based assessment. She adds confidence elicitation
to her weekly quizzes (Y4) and asks students to submit
a "confusion note" with each major assignment (Y5). By week
six she has profiles for each student. She uses those profiles
to decide which students to reach out to proactively, not to
change grades. The second evidence stream is giving her
information she can act on.

**Exercises (3):**
1. (Create) For your primary teaching context, identify the
   two GLP components you will begin observing this term.
   For each: name the specific implementation, name what
   infrastructure it requires, and name what you will do
   differently based on what you observe.
2. (Create) Write a two-sentence explanation of friction traces
   for a skeptical colleague who trusts artifact-based assessment
   and has never heard of the GLP framework.
3. (Evaluate) If you were going to design a one-year pilot of
   process-based assessment in your institution, what would
   success look like at the end of the year? Name three specific
   things you would be able to say that you cannot currently say.

**Closing:**
The artifact used to be proof of the process. It no longer is.
The process leaves traces. The traces are measurable. You do
not need to measure all seven at once. You need to begin.
Start with one question you can ask before every quiz: "How
confident are you in your answer?" Track the gap between
confidence and accuracy. That gap is the first evidence that
genuine learning is being measured differently from performance.
Start there.

# PART 10 — HARD TOPICS, CONTESTED CLAIMS, AGING RISK

## Hard chapters

**Chapter 1:** Must present the Bastani finding accurately without
overgeneralizing. The finding is one RCT in one country in one
subject. The book uses it as the clearest available demonstration
of the engagement-learning gap. Risk: if presented as a settled
finding rather than the strongest available evidence, the book
will be vulnerable to methodological critique.

**Chapter 2:** The neurobiological basis section must be accessible
without being wrong. The dopamine, BDNF, and dendritic spine
formation claims are real but come from studies with different
methodologies and contexts. Must be presented as "why the traces
exist" (mechanistic) not as "here is the clinical evidence"
(empirical claim requiring different support).

**Chapter 10:** The gaming cost argument must be stated precisely.
The claim is not "the framework cannot be gamed" but "gaming all
seven traces simultaneously approaches the cognitive cost of genuine
engagement." The distinction matters for academic credibility.

## Contested claims

| Claim | Status | Book's position |
|---|---|---|
| Bastani 2025 PNAS (unguarded AI harms learning) | One RCT, one country, one subject | "Strongest available evidence"; explicitly flagged as needing replication |
| Neurobiological basis for friction traces | Mechanistic claims from multiple studies, not a unified RCT | Presented as "why the mechanism works," not as direct empirical evidence for GLP |
| GLP seven components are independent evidence | Theoretical claim with strong logical support; limited large-scale validation | Presented as the framework's foundational bet; validation pathway described in Chapter 10 |
| Gaming cost argument | Logical argument, not empirical finding | Presented as a structural claim, not a measured result |

## Aging risk

| Content | Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| AI detection tool landscape | HIGH — changes monthly | Framed at principle level (structural limitations), not product reviews |
| Bastani as anchor finding | MEDIUM — needs replication, field moving fast | Explicitly "strongest available" not "settled" |
| Platform implementations (Medhavy, Canvas) | MEDIUM | Referenced as examples, not as current product descriptions |
| Neurobiological basis | LOW — foundational science is stable | Stable; cited at mechanism level |
| The seven components | LOW — grounded in durable learning science | Stable; Bjork, Vygotsky, cognitive load theory are not going to be overturned |
