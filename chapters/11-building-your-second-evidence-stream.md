# Chapter 11 — Building Your Second Evidence Stream

*You do not need a platform to begin. You need to pick two components, add them to what you already do, and observe what you find.*

---

## Opening case: a starting point, not a destination

You do not need a platform to begin. You need to pick two components, add them to what you already do, and observe what you find. The full seven-component framework is the destination. One component is the starting point. Two is realistic. Three is ambitious for a first term and you should not start there.

This chapter is shorter and more operational than the chapters that came before it, on purpose. Everything in the book up to here has been argument: why the artifact is no longer enough, why genuine learning leaves traces, what each of the seven traces measures, why the seven combine into more than a list. None of that argument is useful if the reader closes the book without picking up two components and trying them. This chapter exists to close that gap. It is the difference between a framework you read about and a framework you used.

What follows is a triage tool — which components are most accessible given your current infrastructure — followed by starting-point recommendations for three teaching contexts, an example of what the first term looks like, an ethics section that the framework owes its users, and a closing action that takes about fifteen seconds to commit to. By the end of the chapter you should have two component choices written down and a first implementation sketched.

**Implication for practice.** Adding two components is a small change to what you already do. It is not a curriculum redesign. It is not a platform purchase. It is two questions you ask, a small log you keep, and a different conversation you have with three or four students by week six. That is the unit of change.

## The triage framework: a component-accessibility ladder

Not all seven components cost the same to implement. Some require nothing beyond a single question added to your existing quizzes. Others require coded analysis of discussion transcripts. The right starting point is not the most informative component. It is the most accessible component for your current infrastructure.

Here is the ladder, from easiest to hardest, with the practitioner-relevant constraints attached.

**Y4 — Uncertainty Calibration. Easiest.** Add one question after each quiz item: "How confident are you in this answer? (Not confident / Somewhat confident / Very confident)." Track the gap between confidence and accuracy over the term. Infrastructure required: a column in your gradebook or a single survey question. Time-to-first-data: one quiz. Interpretive complexity: low — the gap either narrows or it doesn't.

**Y5 (lite) — Social Knowledge Texture as confusion notes. Nearly as easy.** Ask students to submit a one-paragraph "what confused you most about this material" with each major assignment or each weekly module. You are not coding a full SKT rubric. You are reading for texture: specific confusions, real-time position changes, questions that could not have come from a summary. Infrastructure: a paragraph field on existing submissions. Time-to-first-data: one assignment. Interpretive complexity: moderate — you are reading for signal, and developing that reading takes a few weeks.

**Y6 — Retrieval Strength Decay. Easy but requires planning.** Embed one or two questions from previous units into each new quiz, not announced in advance. Track each student's performance on these decay probes across the term. Infrastructure: a question bank tagged by unit. Time-to-first-data: three or four weeks. Interpretive complexity: low — the curve either holds up or it collapses.

**Y3 — Cross-Context Transfer. Moderate.** Design one near-transfer and one far-transfer item per major concept. The far-transfer item deliberately changes the surface features. Score them separately. Infrastructure: thoughtful assessment design, no new tools. Time-to-first-data: one assessment cycle. Interpretive complexity: moderate — the transfer-gap interpretation depends on the specific items.

**Y1 — Temporal Engagement Pattern. Moderate to hard without a platform.** You can approximate Y1 by asking students to annotate problem sets ("checkmark for easy, question mark for hard") and comparing annotations to actual difficulty. Without an LMS that logs sub-paragraph time, the platform version of Y1 is out of reach. Infrastructure: annotated assignments. Interpretive complexity: moderate.

**Y2 — Error Trajectory Coherence. Hard without a misconception map.** Implementable, but only after you have built or borrowed a misconception taxonomy for your topic. Coding errors by misconception type is real work. Infrastructure: a misconception map. Time-to-first-data: depends on whether the map exists. Interpretive complexity: high — the coherence judgment is non-trivial.

**Y7 — Scaffolding Response Curve. Easy for high-touch teaching, hard for large lectures.** Implementable in any context with sustained individual contact (office hours, tutoring, clinical bedside teaching). Requires the two-level hint pair from Chapter 9. Not easily implementable in lecture-only formats with no individual contact. Infrastructure: a two-level hint design per concept. Interpretive complexity: moderate.

Most readers, looking at this ladder, will find that two of these components are very accessible in their current practice. Pick those two. The accessibility ladder is the triage tool. The recommendations that follow apply it to three common contexts.

**Implication for practice.** The biggest implementation failure is choosing components by *theoretical importance* rather than by accessibility. A reader who picks Y2 and Y5 because they sound most diagnostic, and then can't sustain the coding workload, ends up with no data at all. A reader who picks Y4 and Y6 because they are achievable, and then accumulates two terms of useful data, has done the right thing.

## Starting points for different contexts

**K-12, especially high school science (the primary reader of this book).** Start with Y4 (confidence elicitation) and Y5-lite (confusion notes). Both fit inside existing assessment without LMS modification. Y4 lives as a one-question add-on to weekly quizzes. Y5-lite lives as a one-paragraph add-on to each major assignment. Why this pair: Y4 is well-documented in K-12 (the "traffic light" or red-amber-green cards from UK formative-assessment practice, codified in Wiliam (2011), have been deployed in thousands of classrooms). Y5-lite has decades of documented practice as the "one-minute paper" (Angelo and Cross, *Classroom Assessment Techniques*, 1993). Neither implementation is hypothetical. Both have been done at scale and the methods are public. A high school science department chair could implement both across her department this term and have profiles for every student in every class by week six.

**Higher education, lecture-and-section format.** Start with Y6 (decay probes in existing quizzes) and Y3 (near/far transfer pairs in unit assessments). Why this pair: in a higher-ed lecture course, Y4 and Y5-lite are still good but tend to suffer from low response rates and self-selection. Y6 is collected automatically inside graded quizzes — students take them because they count, and you get the decay signal without changing student behavior. Y3 is collected inside unit assessments for the same reason. Both fit inside existing summative infrastructure. Both are robust against the social-desirability bias that contaminates self-reported confidence in adult students. Time-to-first-data is three to four weeks, but the data starts arriving without any new student-facing behavior change.

**Clinical education.** Start with Y7 (structured hint experiment in case presentation and bedside teaching) and Y5 (full SKT rubric on case presentations). Why this pair: clinical education has continuous individual contact and high-touch teaching as the format. Y7 is implementable inside the existing bedside-teaching workflow with no infrastructure beyond writing down the hint level required. Y5 in full form is feasible because case presentations are already verbal artifacts the attending physician is already evaluating — applying the SKT rubric to them is an explicit version of judgment the attending is already making. Y4 confidence elicitation is the natural addition once these two are stable; it has been folded into some residency milestone-assessment frameworks already.

**A note on community college and adult education.** The K-12 starting points apply but with a wrinkle. Adult learners are more likely than K-12 students to under-report confidence (the Dunning-Kruger pattern flips at advanced practitioner levels). Be careful interpreting Y4 in the first month for adult learners. The Y5-lite confusion-note method generally lands cleanly, though, because adult students name confusions specifically when invited to.

**Implication for practice.** Pick the pair that fits your context, not the pair you most admire. If you teach in a context that doesn't appear above — vocational training, music instruction, coaching — the triage logic still applies: look at the ladder, pick the two components your current infrastructure already supports, and start there.

## What the first term looks like

Here is the calendar that most readers can sustain, sketched in weeks for a typical fourteen-week term.

*Weeks 1–2.* Choose your two components. Build the small instrumentation. For Y4 this means adding the confidence question to your quiz template. For Y5-lite it means writing the prompt for the confusion note. For Y6 it means tagging questions in your bank by unit so you can pull decay probes. The instrumentation takes a few hours, not days. Resist building elaborate systems.

*Weeks 3–4.* Collect the first data. Do not analyze it yet. The temptation to look at week-three data and draw conclusions is the most reliable way to draw wrong conclusions. The signals are noisy at first. Confidence calibration looks bad in week three because students do not yet know what to expect. Decay probes have no decay yet because the material is too recent. Sit with the data.

*Week 5.* Look at the data for the first time. Look at the class-level distribution before you look at individual students. Class-level patterns are more reliable than individual-level patterns this early. Note what you see. Do not act yet.

*Weeks 6–8.* Begin individual-level reading. By week six you have three or four weeks of accumulated data per student. Now you can read profiles. Identify the three or four students whose profiles surprise you — the ones whose artifact grades and process signals disagree. Have one conversation with each of them. The conversation is not "I think you're using AI." The conversation is "I noticed [specific pattern] and wanted to understand what you're experiencing on this material." That is a different conversation. Take notes.

*Weeks 9–11.* Refine your reading. By now you have a sense of what the signals are telling you in your specific course. Some signals are louder than others; one of your two components is probably more diagnostic than the other in your context. Adjust if needed. Consider adding a third component if the first two have stabilized — but only if. Resist the temptation to expand too fast.

*Weeks 12–14.* End-of-term reflection. Look at the profile-vs-artifact agreement and disagreement across the class. Note where they agreed, where they diverged, and what the divergences predicted. Compare end-of-term assessments (where artifact-level evidence is strong) to the profile-based predictions you would have made at week six. The framework will calibrate itself to your judgment by the end of the first term. By the second term, you will have a sense of what each signal is worth in your specific classroom.

A note on what to *do* with the data, since this is where most first-term implementations go off the rails. The data is for *conversations with students*, not for *grade calculations*. The framework's stated design is that GLP scores are not grades; the meta-model is the instructor's judgment, and the judgment is exercised at the artifact-and-conversation level, not the algorithmic level. A first-term implementation that uses the data to flag students for outreach, support, and follow-up conversations is doing the right thing. A first-term implementation that uses the data to adjust grades is doing the wrong thing. Wait at least two terms before considering any use of the data for credentialing purposes, and read the ethics section below before you do.

**Implication for practice.** The first term is data collection and pattern-noticing. The second term is acting on what the patterns reliably tell you. Do not collapse the two. The discipline of "collect data this term, act on it next term" is the most important implementation rule the chapter contains.

## The ethics of process observation

The framework asks instructors to collect process data on students. That collection is a moral act and the framework cannot be implemented honestly without addressing it directly. The ethical framing below is approximately fifteen percent of this chapter on purpose. Less, and the framework would be evading a question its users will face. More, and the practical content would be swamped. The proportion follows the practitioner-publication standard that Wiliam (2011) and similar formative-assessment work have used.

**The distinction the framework rests on: observation for learning support vs. observation for surveillance.** These two are not the same activity even when they collect the same data. The difference is *purpose*, *data ownership*, and *what the data is used for*. Observation for learning support has the student's learning as its purpose, makes the data available to the student, and is used to inform teaching decisions in the service of the student. Observation for surveillance has institutional efficiency or accountability as its purpose, makes the data primarily available to institutional actors above the student, and is used to make decisions about the student rather than for her. Selwyn (2019) is the most forceful critic of learning analytics on exactly this distinction; his argument is that good-intentioned process data tends to drift toward surveillance regardless of designer intent. The drift is real. The framework's defense is that the drift is mitigable through deliberate design choices, not that the drift is impossible.

**Who controls the data.** A school or university implementing GLP-style process observation has to decide who has access to which data and under what conditions. The default the framework recommends is: the student has access to her own profile (transparency); the instructor has access to her students' profiles for teaching purposes (pedagogical use); the institution does not have access to individual-student profiles for grading, ranking, or disciplinary purposes (boundary against surveillance use). Aggregate, de-identified data at the institutional level is acceptable. Individual-level data flowing to administrators is the threshold where the framework's design envelope is being exited.

**FERPA implications for K-12 readers.** In US K-12 contexts, the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act gives parents and (over 18) students rights of access to education records. Process observation data collected under GLP is education-record data under FERPA. Schools implementing it need to ensure parents can access their child's profile on request and that the data retention and disclosure rules are aligned with district policy. This is not a barrier to implementation; thousands of districts already maintain formative-assessment data of similar character. It is a checklist item that the chapter recommends running before deployment.

**The chilling-effect risk and how to mitigate it.** A student who knows her every interaction is being scored will perform differently than a student who does not. The risk is that process observation produces guarded, performative engagement instead of the genuine engagement it is meant to measure — a kind of meta-Hawthorne effect that the framework cannot easily escape. Three mitigations help. First, transparency: tell students the framework exists, what it measures, what the data is used for, and what it is not used for. Students who understand the framework as learning support behave differently from students who experience it as surveillance, even with the same data collection. Second, low stakes: keep the data out of grading decisions for at least the first term, and probably permanently for high-stakes summative purposes. Third, student agency: invite students to view their own profiles and to use them for their own self-regulation. Andrade's (2010) work on student self-assessment shows that students who own their assessment data engage differently with the process than students for whom assessment is something done to them.

**The bottom line.** Process observation is legitimate when its purpose is the student's learning, the student has access to the data, the data is not used adversarially, and the framework is transparent. Process observation slides into surveillance when any of those conditions fail. The distinction is in the design, not in the data. Implement carefully and the framework supports student learning. Implement carelessly and the framework becomes the thing it was meant to displace.

## Institutional design implications

A larger argument sits behind the practitioner-level changes this chapter recommends. If process observation produces an evidence stream the artifact does not — and the book's case is that it does — then the institutional infrastructure currently built around the artifact (the gradebook, the transcript, the credential, the accreditation portfolio) is incomplete. Longitudinal process documentation, currently treated as supplementary evidence in the few institutions that collect it at all, has a structural case for being treated as primary evidence alongside the artifact.

The argument has a long history. Wolf (1989) made the case for process portfolios in K-12. Alverno College has been running ability-based assessment with process documentation as core evidence since 1973. Vermont's portfolio assessment program in the 1990s, before the standardized-testing era buried it, was a serious attempt at this kind of infrastructure. None of these efforts succeeded at scale. Tyack and Cuban's (1995) account of why most school reforms fail — the grammar of schooling resists structural change — applies cleanly here. The framework is asking for a substantial change to how institutions document learning, and the field's track record on substantial assessment infrastructure changes is not encouraging.

The chapter is not going to resolve this. The institutional argument is a different book. What the chapter recommends is that the practitioner reader treat the seven-component framework as something she can implement in her own classroom regardless of what her institution does, and that she be patient with the institutional layer. The urgency argument — that AI has made the artifact's exclusive credentialing role untenable, and that institutions will eventually have to respond — is real but slow. The classroom-level argument is implementable now.

**Implication for practice.** Do not wait for institutional reform. Implement at your level. The institutional level will follow once enough classroom-level data exists to make the case undeniable.

## Worked example: a community college instructor's first semester

A community college English instructor — call her Maria — is in her first semester of process-based assessment. She has read the book and has chosen two components: Y4 (confidence elicitation) on weekly quizzes and Y5-lite (a one-paragraph confusion note submitted with each major assignment). She has fourteen weeks and twenty-six students. *(Illustrative case — composite of community-college formative-assessment implementations from Wiliam (2011) practitioner literature; not traceable to a single documented field deployment of the GLP framework in its current form.)*

Weeks 1 and 2: she adds the confidence question to her quiz template ("How confident are you in your answer? Not / Somewhat / Very"). She writes the confusion-note prompt: "In a paragraph, tell me what part of the reading or the assignment most confused you, and why." She tells the class what the additions are for: "I am collecting these because they help me understand what you are actually experiencing on the material, beyond what your essays alone tell me. I will use them to decide where to spend my office hours and what to clarify in lecture. They are not graded." (This is the transparency move from the ethics section.)

Weeks 3 and 4: data starts coming in. The first round of confidence ratings looks noisy — high confidence everywhere, no pattern. The first round of confusion notes is uneven — some students name specific confusions ("I don't see why the narrator is unreliable in chapter 3 when she gives factual information that turns out to be true"), and some submit generic statements ("It was mostly clear but a few things were hard"). Maria resists analyzing yet.

Week 5: she looks at the data for the first time. At the class level, confidence is uniformly high — typical of week-five undergraduate behavior. At the individual level, confusion-note texture is dividing students into roughly three groups: those producing specific confusions (about a third of the class), those producing generic statements (about a third), and those somewhere in the middle (about a third). She notes the distribution.

Weeks 6 to 8: she begins individual-level reading. By week six she has three quizzes of confidence data and three confusion notes per student. Some patterns are emerging. Two students with strong essay grades have flatly generic confusion notes — no specific encounter with the material visible. Three students with weaker essay grades have specific, textured confusions that suggest genuine engagement with the readings beyond what the essays are showing. She reaches out to all five for conversations. With the two strong-essay/generic-note students, she asks about their reading process — not adversarially, but curiously. She is gathering information. With the three weaker-essay/textured-note students, she invites them to office hours to work on essay drafting, because the confusion notes suggest the underlying engagement is there and the essay is the bottleneck.

Weeks 9 to 11: by now Maria's reading of the data has sharpened. She finds the confusion notes more diagnostic than the confidence ratings in her course, because writing courses are higher-stakes social-cognition tier work where the Y5-family signals load most heavily. She continues collecting Y4 but is putting more weight on Y5-lite. She has not added a third component. She does not need to.

Weeks 12 to 14: end-of-term reflection. The students whose generic confusion notes worried her in week six largely matched her end-of-term concerns. The students whose textured notes suggested hidden strength largely improved their essays once Maria targeted office hours at draft revision. The framework gave her information she would not otherwise have had. It did not change anyone's grade. It changed her teaching.

This worked example is illustrative rather than documentary. The community-college instructor described above is a composite drawn from published formative-assessment implementations of Y4-style confidence elicitation (Wiliam 2011) and Y5-lite-style confusion notes (Angelo and Cross 1993; Cross 1993). Each individual component has documented deployment evidence in K-12 and higher education. The full *seven-component* GLP framework does not yet have published field-deployment cases at the level required to make this example documentary rather than composite. The book flags this honestly: the components are documented; the framework as a whole is in its first deployment phase. Maria is plausible. She is not yet citable.

**Implication for practice.** Your first term will look something like Maria's. Some of the data will be noisy. Some of it will surprise you. Most of it will give you something to act on by week six. The framework's job is not to be precise. Its job is to give you information the artifact alone is not giving you, and to keep doing that reliably enough that you trust it by the end of the term.

## Exercises

**1. (Create) Your first-term implementation plan.** For your primary teaching context, identify the two GLP components you will begin observing this term. For each: name the specific implementation (the exact question, prompt, or protocol you will add), name the infrastructure required (gradebook column, paragraph field, question-bank tag, etc.), and name what you will *do differently* based on what you observe. The last item is the most important. A component you implement but cannot act on is a component you should not implement. Write this exercise as a one-page plan you could hand to a colleague.

**2. (Create) The two-sentence explanation.** Write a two-sentence explanation of friction traces for a skeptical colleague who trusts artifact-based assessment and has never heard of the GLP framework. The two sentences must accomplish three things: name the problem (the artifact is no longer the proxy it was), name the response (observable behavioral traces of genuine learning), and be specific enough that your colleague's first question is "what kinds of traces?" rather than "what are you talking about?" Write three drafts. Keep the best one. (This is the test of whether you can carry the framework into your department.)

**3. (Evaluate) A one-year pilot.** If you were going to design a one-year pilot of process-based assessment in your institution, what would success look like at the end of the year? Name *three specific things* you would be able to say at the end of the year that you cannot currently say. The three things should be empirical (not "students are more engaged" but "students who showed genuine Y4 calibration trajectories had higher delayed retention than students who didn't"). They should be defensible to a skeptical dean. They should be falsifiable. If the year ended and your three things turned out to be false, you would know the framework had failed in your context — and that is what makes them useful as pilot success criteria.

## What would change my mind

The chapter's central practical claim is that two components, well-chosen, produce useful information that the artifact alone does not. If a careful field study across several institutions and several disciplines showed that two-component implementations produced no actionable information beyond what the artifact already provided — that the profiles either agreed with artifact judgments (in which case they added nothing) or disagreed in non-systematic ways (in which case they were just noise) — the chapter's case for implementation would collapse. The Wiliam-tradition formative-assessment literature gives strong reason to believe well-chosen process observation does produce actionable information. The specific GLP-component selection has not been validated at field-study scale. The chapter is exposed to the result of that study when it is done.

A second exposure: the ethics section claims that observation-for-learning-support and observation-for-surveillance are distinguishable in practice. Selwyn's critique is that they drift toward each other regardless of designer intent. If a longitudinal study of GLP-style implementations showed that observation-for-learning consistently drifted into observation-for-grading or observation-for-disciplinary-purposes within a few terms — even when designers explicitly resisted the drift — the chapter's ethical framing would need to be more pessimistic. The honest reading of the existing learning-analytics literature is that the drift is real and that mitigation is hard. The chapter argues mitigation is possible but does not pretend it is automatic.

## Still puzzling

- The right number of components for first-time practitioners. The chapter recommends two. The reasoning is implementation feasibility, not validated power. If empirical work showed that one component, well-implemented, captures most of the diagnostic value for first-time practitioners, the recommendation would change. The two-component recommendation is a bet on motivation and instrumentation overhead, not on signal strength.
- Whether the framework works as well in disciplines its authors have not deeply considered. The book is built from examples drawn primarily from STEM, writing, and clinical education. The framework's portability to art, music, vocational training, physical education, and other non-text-heavy fields is plausible but unvalidated.
- Whether the implementation pattern (collect first term, act second term) survives contact with administrative pressure. Department chairs, deans, and accreditors all want results quickly. Holding the data without acting on it for a term is a discipline that institutional pressures actively undermine. The chapter is recommending a practice that contradicts how most institutional reform cycles work.
- The role of student voice in framework adoption. The book has been written for the instructor's perspective. Students themselves, especially adult learners, have important things to say about being observed in process, and the framework's design has not yet absorbed enough student-voice input. Future editions need to.

## The AI Wayback Machine: Dylan Wiliam

Dylan Wiliam, of the UCL Institute of Education in London, is the figure whose work most directly anticipates this chapter's argument that small, well-chosen instrumentation changes — exit tickets, traffic-light cards, mini-whiteboards, confidence ratings — can produce learning evidence that Canvas dashboards cannot. *Embedded Formative Assessment* (2011) is the practitioner translation of the Black and Wiliam (1998) meta-analysis showing that formative assessment, well-designed, produces effect sizes of 0.4 to 0.7 on student learning. The book is the most-likely-already-on-the-shelf reference for the high school department chair who is the primary reader of this book. Wiliam's argument was that small assessment changes have outsized impact, that the change has to be embedded in routine practice rather than added as an extra layer, and that the teacher remains the integrative layer for the evidence. That is exactly the argument this chapter makes. The framework is, in this sense, formative assessment for the AI era: a translation of Wiliam's structural insight into the specific evidence streams that the post-AI assessment problem requires. Wiliam would recognize Y4 and Y5-lite as direct descendants of the practices he has been advocating since the 1990s. The book's contribution is not the formative-assessment principle. It is the specific seven-component instantiation of the principle that the decoupling problem now demands.

## Closing: start there

The artifact used to be proof of the process. It no longer is. The process leaves traces. The traces are measurable. You do not need to measure all seven at once. You need to begin.

Start with one question you can ask before every quiz: "How confident are you in your answer?" Track the gap between confidence and accuracy. That gap is the first evidence that genuine learning is being measured differently from performance.

Start there.
