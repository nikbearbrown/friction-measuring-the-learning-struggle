# Chapter 2 — What Genuine Learning Leaves Behind

*Why genuine cognitive engagement produces behavioral traces that borrowed certainty cannot — and why those traces are the basis of the seven-component framework.*

---

## Opening case: the traces are not a metaphor

When this book uses the phrase "friction traces," it is not reaching for a literary effect. The traces are behavioral consequences of neurobiological events that constitute genuine learning. A student who offloaded her essay to an AI did not trigger the prediction errors, the BDNF expression, or the dendritic spine formation that genuine engagement with difficult material produces. The AI cannot produce the behavioral traces those events leave, because the events did not occur. The keystrokes happened. The cognition did not.

This is the load-bearing claim of the chapter, and it has to be handled honestly. The mechanisms are real. The mapping from mechanism to friction trace is a mechanistic argument, not a body of clinical evidence directly about essay-writing or quiz performance. The three paragraphs of neurobiology in this chapter are meant to explain *why* the traces exist as a matter of how the brain learns — not to claim that the studies in question have been run on classroom assessment with AI-assisted students.

That distinction matters because the framework's credibility depends on it. The seven-component apparatus rests on durable learning science — Bjork, Sweller, Vygotsky, the prediction-error literature — combined with the mechanistic story below. The story tells you why the apparatus should work. The empirical case is still being built, and the GLP preprint is where that case is made formally. This chapter is the *why-the-mechanism-works* layer.

**Implication for practice.** When you tell a skeptical colleague why process traces are real, anchor on the mechanisms. When you tell her that "the traces have been validated empirically across all seven components in K-12 settings," you have over-claimed. The mechanisms are what the field knows about how learning works. The seven-component framework is the synthesis claim that converts mechanism into measurement.

## Why genuine learning leaves traces: three mechanisms in plain language

**Prediction error and dopamine.** Schultz, Dayan, and Montague (1997) demonstrated that dopamine neurons in the midbrain fire when an outcome is *better than expected*, drop below baseline when an outcome is *worse than expected*, and fall silent when outcomes match expectation. The signal is not "reward." The signal is "prediction error" — the gap between what was expected and what occurred. The cellular mechanics are well replicated. In a learning context, the moment of being wrong (or of being unexpectedly right) is the moment the system updates. The student who reads the AI's correct answer before generating her own does not produce that gap. There is nothing to update against. The mechanism that *makes learning happen* didn't fire.

**BDNF under effortful demand.** Brain-derived neurotrophic factor is a molecule that supports synaptic plasticity — the neural change that learning physically is. The cleanest evidence for BDNF upregulation comes from aerobic-exercise studies (Cotman and Berchtold, 2002), and the field's careful claim is that BDNF mediates plasticity and responds to *effortful demand* generally. The honest framing for this book is: when cognitive demand is genuine and within reach, the neurochemistry that supports lasting change is engaged. When the demand is offloaded, it is not. This is mechanism, not direct evidence that "writing an essay upregulates BDNF."

**Dendritic spine formation.** Yang, Pan, and Gan (2009) imaged living mouse motor cortex while the mice learned a motor task. They watched new dendritic spines — the tiny protrusions where synapses live — form rapidly, and they watched the spines that *persisted* predict which memories the mice retained over time. The work is mouse motor cortex, not human declarative learning, and the chapter says that out loud. What the work tells us, mechanistically, is that durable memory has a *structural* substrate that takes effortful engagement to build and that this substrate is what makes the memory persist. The student who has read the AI's answer has formed no such substrate for the content she did not actually process.

**Implication for practice.** You do not need the neuroscience to use the framework. You need it because the framework's central claim — that the traces exist independent of the artifact — rests on the fact that *something physically happens when learning occurs that does not happen when it doesn't.* That is the warrant. It does not require you to point at a particular synapse to be valid.

## Storage strength and retrieval strength: the Bjork distinction in plain language

The mechanism that has held longest in learning science is Bjork's two-factor model. Anything in memory has two strengths attached to it. *Retrieval strength* is how accessible the thing is right now — how easily it comes to mind when prompted. *Storage strength* is how thoroughly it is encoded — how durable it is, how well it will hold up two weeks from now, three months from now, against decay. The two factors are partially independent: you can have high retrieval strength and low storage strength (you can answer the question on the quiz today and not remember it next term) and you can have lower immediate retrieval and high storage strength (the thing is in there; it just takes a moment to come up).

This distinction is fifty years old and still the cleanest way to explain why most assessment is partially blind. Most assessment measures retrieval strength at one timepoint, close to instruction. That number is the easiest one to collect and the worst predictor of what the student will carry into next year. Storage strength is what assessment is *supposed* to evidence. It can only be observed across time, against decay, or under conditions that demand effortful retrieval rather than easy recognition.

Karpicke and Roediger (2008) ran the cleanest version of this. Students studied a passage and were asked to predict which study method — re-reading or self-testing — would help them remember the material a week later. Students confidently predicted re-reading. The data said the opposite: self-testing produced substantially better one-week retention. The students were not stupid. They were reading *retrieval strength* and calling it *learning*. Re-reading feels fluent. Fluency feels like understanding. Fluency is retrieval strength. Retrieval strength is not storage strength. The student who feels she "gets it" can be exactly right at the level of retrieval and exactly wrong at the level of storage.

**Implication for practice.** Every assessment in your current practice is measuring some mixture of retrieval and storage strength, and the mixture leans toward retrieval. When you add AI access to that picture, the retrieval-strength signal stops being the student's at all — the system is doing the retrieval. Storage-strength evidence becomes the only thing left that is actually about the student. The traces this book describes are mostly storage-strength evidence and metacognitive evidence about that storage. They are not "extras." They are the part of learning that is now most directly the student's, and least directly the AI's.

## The fluency trap

The Karpicke and Roediger result lives in your students' assessment behavior every term. The version that matters for this book is the AI version of it.

AI-generated text is, by design, fluent. It is also confidently structured, syntactically clean, and consistent in tone. To a reader, fluency is a strong heuristic for understanding — Bender and colleagues (2021) called this the "stochastic parrots" problem from the model's side, but the same trap operates on the consumer of the model's output. A clear, confident, well-organized explanation produces in the reader the same feeling that a clear, confident, well-organized internal model would produce. Fluency feels like understanding because, before AI, fluent expression usually was a downstream consequence of understanding. The proxy used to work for the same reason artifact-as-proof used to work: there was no other supplier.

Now there is. The student who reads the AI's clear explanation experiences fluency. Fluency feels like learning. The student forms the metacognitive impression that she has understood. The impression is wrong, but it is the same impression she has had her whole education, because every previous time she felt fluent reading something, the fluency was earned. The AI has trained her metacognition to trust a signal that no longer means what it used to mean.

This has a counter-intuitive implication for reading student work. *High artifact polish, in isolation, is now mild negative evidence of genuine engagement.* The rougher artifact — the one with structural seams, the one where you can see a draft that abandoned a paragraph, the one where the student lost the thread and found it again — is often the *more* diagnostic artifact. It is the one that shows the cognition. The polished artifact may show the cognition or may show the absence of it. The polish is no longer signal in either direction.

**Implication for practice.** Train yourself, and your colleagues, against the instinct that says "the polished one must be the better student." Polish was a proxy for cognition. It is now ambiguous. The artifact that shows the *process* of having worked something out is the artifact you can read confidently. The artifact that arrives smooth and finished may be the work of a strong student or of a competent prompter; you can't tell from polish alone.

## What "friction" is, and why it isn't a problem to be removed

The instinct that runs through a lot of educational design — and almost all educational technology — is that friction is a defect. The student is confused; the platform smooths the confusion. The problem is hard; the tool removes the hardness. The grading is slow; automation speeds it up. Some of this is right. Most of it isn't.

Bjork and Bjork (2011) collected the case for *desirable difficulties*: spacing practice rather than massing it; interleaving topics rather than blocking them; varying the conditions of practice; requiring retrieval rather than re-presentation. Every one of these makes the student feel like she is learning *less*. Every one of them produces durable learning *more*. The feeling of fluent acquisition correlates negatively with the production of storage strength. Difficulty is not the obstacle to learning. Difficulty is the *thing*. The struggle is not the symptom of bad pedagogy; it is the mechanism. Sweller's cognitive-load community frames the same point in different language — load is real, load is a problem when it is *extraneous* (the worksheet is confusing for reasons unrelated to the content), but the load that is *intrinsic* to the material's element-interactivity is exactly the load you want the student carrying.

This matters for the framework because every trace the book is about to introduce is a trace of *productive friction*. Time bunching on hard problems (Y1) is the trace of cognitive load distributing where the difficulty actually is. Errors that follow a coherent path through misconception space (Y2) are the trace of a mental model updating against prediction error. Transfer to new surface features (Y3) is the trace of a schema that abstracted away from the original context. Calibration improving over time (Y4) is the trace of a metacognitive monitor that has been encountering reality. Texture in discussion (Y5) is the trace of having actually encountered the material. Decay curves that hold up (Y6) are the storage-strength trace. Hint-response patterns (Y7) are the trace of a developing model that a partial cue can engage.

When the student avoids friction by routing the task through the AI, no friction occurs. No trace forms. The artifact comes out the other side. The cognition does not. The traces are absent because the events that produce them did not happen.

**Implication for practice.** The pedagogical question is no longer "how do I reduce friction?" The pedagogical question is "how do I make sure the friction the student needs is friction she actually meets?" Some AI-assisted designs (Kestin's PEAR tutor is the cleanest published example) accomplish exactly that — they hold the friction in front of the student and offer scaffolding without offering the solution. Some don't. The artifact does not tell you which is which; the traces do.

## The seven components — preview

The book builds out seven friction traces. Each one is the behavioral signature of one or more of the mechanisms above. Each one is observable, on a small scale, without a platform. The combination is what makes the framework hard to game; no single trace, on its own, is bulletproof. Here are the seven, in one sentence each.

- **Y1 — Temporal Engagement Pattern.** Whether time-on-task correlates with item difficulty, the way cognitive-load theory predicts it must when the student is the one bearing the load.
- **Y2 — Error Trajectory Coherence.** Whether the student's errors cluster around identifiable misconceptions and shift coherently as the mental model updates, the way prediction-error-driven learning predicts they should.
- **Y3 — Cross-Context Transfer.** Whether understanding survives a change in surface features, the way an abstract schema (rather than a pattern-matched template) predicts it must.
- **Y4 — Uncertainty Calibration.** Whether expressed confidence tracks actual performance, the way effortful encoding predicts it should for a learner who has met her own limits.
- **Y5 — Social Knowledge Texture.** Whether discussion and response carry the specific marks of genuine encounter — particular confusions, real-time position changes, productive uncertainty — that are different from the smooth generic surface that borrowed certainty produces.
- **Y6 — Retrieval Strength Decay Signature.** Whether performance holds up against time and against the spacing manipulation that distinguishes storage strength from retrieval strength.
- **Y7 — Scaffolding Response Curve.** Whether the student responds to partial hints as someone with partial understanding would — versus needing the full answer because no underlying model exists for a hint to engage.

Each trace, on its own, can be faked or noisy. The argument the book makes in Chapter 10 is that faking all seven simultaneously approaches the cost of actually learning. The combination is the second evidence stream. The combination is what you read alongside the artifact.

**Implication for practice.** You do not need to observe all seven to begin. Most readers of this book will start with one or two — typically Y4 (a confidence-elicitation question added to existing quizzes, the cheapest of the seven) or Y1 (a self-rated difficulty annotation on problem sets). Chapter 11 walks through the starting point. Chapters 3 through 9 walk through each component in turn.

## Worked example: two students, one assignment

Student A is assigned a five-page essay on the causes of the French Revolution. Over a week, her drafting record shows three drafts. The first is rough — paragraphs in the wrong order, two false starts, a place around the middle where she clearly lost the thread and came back to it from a different angle. The second draft has reorganized those paragraphs and cut about a page of material that didn't earn its place. The final draft is cleaner but still has a paragraph her teacher can read as the one where she was still working something out. The whole thing took maybe three hours of writing time, spread across four sittings. She is in office hours twice during the week with specific questions — one about whether economic causes should come before or after political ones, one about how to handle Rousseau given that she's not sure she understands him as well as the rest of her sources.

Student B turns in a single five-page document at the deadline. There are no intermediate drafts in the version history. The total time the document was open is about twenty minutes. The writing is fluent. The structure is clean. The argument is competent. He is not in office hours. There are no specific questions.

Both essays are about the same quality on the artifact axis. A teacher grading on artifact alone would mark them similarly.

Now read the friction traces. Student A shows time spent (a lot of it), spent unevenly across the work (Y1-like — the messy middle paragraph took longer than the structured opening), evidence of error-revision (paragraphs cut, false starts abandoned — Y2-like), and specific confusions she could articulate (the Rousseau question — Y5-like). Student B shows none of these. Student B's behavior around the artifact is consistent with the artifact having been produced by something that doesn't make false starts, doesn't ask questions about Rousseau, and doesn't take three hours to do five pages.

This is not proof Student B used AI. It is, however, an information asymmetry the teacher can read. The teacher who looks only at the artifact sees two competent essays. The teacher who looks at the artifact *and* the behavior around it sees one student whose process is in the room and another student whose process is, at minimum, somewhere the teacher cannot see. That second observation is exactly the additional evidence the framework is built to make systematic.

**Implication for practice.** You have probably already made this comparison informally — about a student whose in-class writing doesn't match her take-home writing, or whose discussion contributions don't match her essay quality. The framework gives you a name for what you are seeing and a way to do the comparison on purpose rather than by accident.

## Exercises

**1. (Understand) Your own genuine struggle.** Pick a topic you genuinely struggled to understand at some point in your education — something where the click into understanding took real effort. Describe, in three or four sentences, the *behavioral traces* your struggle left: what you did, how long it took, what you tried that didn't work, when you knew it had clicked. Now describe what those traces would have looked like if, at the start, you had read a clear AI explanation of the topic and felt that you understood it. The contrast is the chapter's argument written in your own handwriting.

**2. (Apply) The trace you've been reading without naming.** Name one piece of student behavior you have noticed — across years of teaching — that was *not in the artifact* but told you whether genuine learning was occurring. Don't reach for a recent example; use the one that comes to mind first. Which of the seven previewed components does it most closely match? The answer is the entry point into the rest of the book.

**3. (Understand) The fluency trap, two sentences.** Explain the fluency trap to a skeptical colleague in exactly two sentences. The colleague does not need to know what AI is for the explanation to land. The constraint forces you to state the mechanism — fluent expression feels like understanding to a reader because in the world your colleague grew up in, fluency was usually downstream of understanding — without reaching for AI-specific examples. The test of the explanation is whether your colleague says, "Yes, I've seen that with student summaries of textbook chapters too."

## What would change my mind

The chapter's central claim is that genuine learning produces behavioral traces that borrowed certainty cannot, because the underlying neurobiological events did not occur in the borrowed case. The claim would need substantial revision if a body of evidence emerged showing that *reading* a clear AI explanation reliably produces the same downstream learning indicators — durable retention, transfer, calibration — as *working through* the material would have produced. The honest version of the chapter's bet is that there is something irreducible about *doing the cognition* that reading a clean explanation does not substitute for. If that turns out to be wrong — if it turns out that, for many tasks, well-designed AI explanations produce learning indistinguishable from effortful encoding — the framework's premise weakens. The current evidence (Bastani 2025, Karpicke and Roediger 2008, the desirable-difficulties literature broadly) runs the other way, but the question is empirical and remains open.

## Still puzzling

- How much neurobiological detail is actually *load-bearing* for a practitioner's confidence in the framework? Three paragraphs feels right; less may leave the reader uncertain why the mechanism story is more than vibes; more may push her into research mode and lose her. The right calibration is the chapter's open question.
- The fluency-trap literature is mostly about text re-reading. Is there literature specifically on AI-generated-explanation fluency illusions? At the time of writing, the literature is thin to absent. This is a gap the framework's empirical case will need to fill.
- Where is the boundary between *desirable* difficulty and *destructive* difficulty in practice? Bjork's framework distinguishes the two in theory, but a practicing teacher has to make the call in the moment. Better operational heuristics would help.
- The book treats the seven traces as partially independent. They aren't fully independent — Y2 (error trajectory) and Y4 (calibration) plausibly co-vary. How much of the framework's diagnostic power survives if some of the components turn out to be more correlated than the framework currently treats them?
