# Chapter 7 — Y5: Social Knowledge Texture

*Genuine encounter with material produces specific confusions, real-time position changes, and productive uncertainty in discussion. Borrowed certainty produces smooth generic statements. The texture is the signal.*

---

## Opening: the two answers in the seminar room

An instructor asks a seminar question. Two students answer in sequence.

The first says: "I think the mechanism works through X, but I'm confused about why Y doesn't interfere. When Y is present, you'd expect the second pathway to dominate — but the textbook says it doesn't. Either I'm misunderstanding what Y is doing, or there's a step we haven't covered yet. Can you help me see which one?"

The second says: "The mechanism is a complex interplay of multiple factors working in conjunction to produce the observed outcomes. There are many variables involved, and different perspectives offer different insights into the underlying processes."

Both students answered. Both used full sentences. Both spoke for roughly the same amount of time.

The instructor knows the difference immediately. So do you. So would anyone who has ever taught a discussion section.

The first student is *thinking*. The thinking is visible. It has texture. The confusion is specific — Y, the second pathway, the textbook's claim. The student is anchoring the confusion to something they tried to do and could not complete. The question is genuine. It could not have been generated without the attempt.

The second student is *performing*. The sentences are well-formed and content-free. They could have been spoken without reading the chapter. They could have been generated by an AI in two seconds. The texture is absent. The smoothness *is* the absence.

This recognition is not a research finding. It is a daily competence every experienced teacher has and most cannot defend in writing. Y5 is the chapter where we name the recognition, formalize the recognition into a four-marker rubric, and acknowledge — honestly — that this is the most subjective of the seven signals, and that the rubric is a practitioner tool, not a validated psychometric instrument.

The chapter has three jobs. Make the recognition explicit. Give you a usable rubric. Tell you what the rubric is not.

---

## What Social Knowledge Texture measures

**Y5 — Social Knowledge Texture (SKT)** [Humanitarians AI internal framework] is the fifth friction-trace component. It measures the texture of a student's contribution to discussion — written or spoken — along four markers. Each marker is the kind of detail that is hard to manufacture without having genuinely encountered the material.

**Marker 1 — Personal encounter.** Does the student name a specific confusion they had, with enough detail that you can see what they tried to do and where it broke down? Specificity is the signal. "I'm confused" is generic. "I'm confused about why the d-dimer was normal in a patient with all the risk factors for PE" is specific. Specificity comes from having actually attempted the task.

**Marker 2 — Below-surface engagement.** Does the contribution connect to prior knowledge in a way that emerged from the student's own struggle? Not "this reminds me of last week's chapter" — that's recitation. "This reminds me of the equilibrium problem we did but in this case the temperature isn't constant, so I'm not sure Le Chatelier applies the same way" — that's a connection the student made by trying to apply, failing, and noticing.

**Marker 3 — Productive uncertainty.** Does the contribution contain questions that could only have arisen from real engagement? "What is this concept?" is not productive uncertainty. "If this concept applies, then I'd predict X — but the data show Y. Either the concept doesn't apply here, or my prediction is wrong" is. The productive question is anchored, contestable, and a bid for help that an answer could actually resolve.

**Marker 4 — Real-time development.** Does the student's position change during the conversation itself? Genuine engagement is dynamic. The student starts somewhere, encounters a counter-argument, and ends somewhere else, and can name what shifted. Borrowed certainty does not develop. The student arrives with a rehearsed position and leaves with the same one.

The lineage of these four markers runs through Neil Mercer's work on **exploratory talk** (Mercer and Littleton 2007) — the talk that engages with other people's ideas, makes reasoning explicit, and accepts that positions can change. It runs through **Resnick's accountable talk** (Resnick, Asterhan, and Clarke 2015) — talk that is accountable to the community, to accurate knowledge, and to rigorous thinking. It runs through **Soter et al. (2008)** — the meta-analytic identification of authentic open-ended questions, uptake, and elaborated explanations as the discourse features that predict comprehension. And it runs through **Howe et al. (2019)** — the large UK primary-classroom study confirming that specific dialogic moves (querying, building, contesting) predict end-of-year learning at scale.

The four markers are a synthesis aimed at one practitioner question: can a teacher score this contribution in 90 seconds and reach roughly the same answer as a colleague who scored it the same way? They are not a validated psychometric instrument. They have not been subjected to formal inter-rater-reliability testing. The chapter is using "rubric" in the practitioner sense — a structured checklist — not in the assessment-research sense.

**Practitioner implication:** the recognition is older than the rubric. The rubric just makes the recognition shareable across teachers in the same school.

## Where texture comes from

Why does genuine encounter produce texture and borrowed certainty produce smoothness? The mechanism is at least three things at once.

**The first is structural.** Genuine attempt to apply a concept generates specific failures. The student tries to predict what happens when the temperature changes; the prediction doesn't match the data; the student now has a question that is anchored to a specific moment in their reasoning. The question's specificity is the residue of the attempt. AI does not generate that residue, because the AI does not attempt anything. The AI describes.

**The second is sociocultural.** Mercer's work, building on Vygotsky, treats discussion as a cognitive event the group does together. The student who is genuinely engaging is doing some of their thinking *out loud, in the discussion*. The position changes, the new questions, the partial answers that get refined as the conversation proceeds — these are the thinking happening. AI-generated contributions are finished products. They do not develop because they are not the residue of in-progress cognition; they are post-hoc summaries with no in-progress phase.

**The third is the fluency trap from Chapter 2, in its most acute form.** Written AI prose is structurally indistinguishable from careful student prose at the sentence level. Both are grammatical. Both are clear. Both contain content. What the AI cannot produce is the *anchoring* — the specific detail that ties the contribution to a real attempt at a real task. The AI can write "I'm confused about X" if asked. The AI cannot reliably write "I'm confused because I tried Y and got Z, but my friend tried Y and got W, and I think one of us made an error in step three but I can't tell which." The second sentence requires having done Y. The first does not.

Two related ideas from the learning sciences. **Scardamalia and Bereiter's distinction between knowledge-telling and knowledge-building** (1994): knowledge-telling is reporting what you know; knowledge-building is collectively advancing the community's understanding through specific moves (problems of understanding, building on ideas, rise-above). Knowledge-building produces texture; knowledge-telling does not. **Garrison, Anderson, and Archer's Community of Inquiry framework** (2001), which gives cognitive presence four phases: triggering event, exploration, integration, resolution. Borrowed certainty tends to get stuck in exploration — naming the topic — without ever integrating or resolving.

**Practitioner implication:** texture is not stylistic. It is structural. It is what survives when you remove the prose and ask "does this contribution depend on the student having actually done something specific?"

## The genuine SKT signature

When a student is genuinely engaging with the material, the four markers tend to co-occur. Not always all four — a single short contribution may show one or two — but the pattern across multiple contributions is clear:

**Specific confusions named with anchoring detail.** "The cell respiration unit — I get how electrons move through the chain but I keep losing track of where the protons are coming from in chemiosmosis." The student names what they understand, what they don't, and what part of the chapter they got stuck on.

**Connections that emerged during the student's own struggle.** "When you talked about natural selection in moths, I kept thinking about how my doctor said she was worried about antibiotic resistance — is that the same mechanism?" The student is bringing material from outside the class and using it to test the principle. The connection is theirs.

**Questions that have a structure.** Not "I don't get it" but "If A is true then I'd expect B, but I'm seeing C — which is wrong?" The structure shows the student tried to predict an outcome.

**Position change during the conversation.** "I thought the answer was X because of Y, but when Marcus pointed out Z, I realized Y doesn't actually rule out the other case. So now I think it's either X or W and I don't know how to decide between them." This is the most diagnostic of the four markers because it is the hardest to fake — it requires real-time response to an utterance that wasn't predicted in advance.

**Practitioner implication:** the genuine signature is messier than the borrowed-certainty signature. Texture is not the same as polish.

## The borrowed-certainty SKT signature

The pattern is uniform and clean and it has gotten worse since 2023.

**Generic statements without anchoring.** "There are many factors to consider." "It's a complex topic." "Different perspectives offer different insights." Anyone who has spent time reading AI-generated discussion posts recognizes this register. The sentences are grammatical. The sentences are content-free.

**No real-time development.** The contribution is the same length and the same tone from the first sentence to the last. The student does not respond to what was said before them — or responds with a paraphrase that adds no information. There is no shift mid-contribution because there is no in-progress cognition to shift.

**Rehearsed positions that do not move under challenge.** Asked a follow-up — "but what about case X?" — the student either repeats their original position with new words or pivots to a new topic. The position cannot move because there is no underlying reasoning for the challenge to engage.

**No specific confusions.** Asked what was hard, the student says "all of it" or "the complexity." When pressed, they cannot produce a specific moment of struggle. There is no specific moment because there was no struggle.

There is an emergent pattern in 2024–2026 [verify] worth naming: many practitioners report that asynchronous discussion forums have functionally collapsed as learning environments since late 2022. Contributions read uniformly fluent and uniformly generic. The platform was designed assuming a distribution of voices; the platform now receives an undifferentiated distribution of AI-mediated voices. Y5 is the chapter where this collapse is visible at the level of the individual contribution.

A failure mode the chapter must name: the **trained performer**. A student who has learned to *produce* Y5-shaped contributions without genuinely engaging is conceivable. They have learned the rhetorical moves — name a specific confusion, change positions, ask a structured question — and execute them strategically. The defense against this is the ensemble (Chapter 10). A student who produces convincing Y5 texture and convincing Y2 error coherence and convincing Y3 transfer is, by construction, learning. The ensemble makes the fake too expensive.

**Practitioner implication:** borrowed certainty in discussion is structurally bland. The blandness is the signal.

## How to observe Y5 without a platform — the SKT rubric

This is where the chapter must be most concrete. The rubric is a practitioner tool, not a validated instrument. It exists to make a teacher's existing recognition shareable, applicable in 90 seconds, and explainable to a colleague.

**The SKT rubric. Score each marker 0–2. Total score: 0–8.**

| Marker | 0 (absent) | 1 (present but weak) | 2 (clearly present) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Personal encounter** | No specific confusion named. Generic statements only. | Confusion mentioned but not anchored to a specific attempt. ("I found this confusing.") | Specific confusion anchored to a specific attempted task. ("When I tried to apply X to Y, I got Z and couldn't tell why.") |
| **2. Below-surface engagement** | No connection to prior knowledge. Recitation of given material only. | Connection mentioned but textbook-shaped. ("This reminds me of last chapter.") | Connection emerges from a personal attempt to apply. Brings outside material in productively. |
| **3. Productive uncertainty** | No questions, or questions that are pure information-requests ("What is X?") | Questions present but structurally generic. ("How does this work?") | Questions are anchored, contestable, and an actual answer would resolve them. ("If A is true then B; but I see C; one of us is wrong, which?") |
| **4. Real-time development** | No position change. Static contribution. | Position changes but in a way that paraphrases rather than develops. | Position changes and the student names what changed and why. Visible in-progress cognition. |

A note on Marker 4 in **asynchronous** contexts. Real-time development is the only marker that depends on synchronous interaction. In an asynchronous discussion post, you can sometimes still see it if the post is written *after* the student has read earlier replies and explicitly engages with them ("Marcus's point made me rethink..."). For pure first-post asynchronous contributions, the marker is often unmeasurable and the maximum score effectively becomes 0–6. The chapter recommends flagging this when you report Y5 scores from forum data — note whether the contribution was in a context where Marker 4 was observable.

**A scored example. Discussion prompt: "What is the most confusing thing about cellular respiration?"**

*Contribution A (Student M):*
> "I get how the electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and I get that ATP synthase uses the gradient. What I keep losing track of is *why* the gradient drives ATP synthesis specifically — couldn't the cell just use the proton gradient directly for other purposes? Is ATP just the most efficient currency, or is there a structural reason it has to be ATP? I tried to look this up and got contradictory explanations."

Marker 1 (personal encounter): 2 — anchored to a specific attempt to look it up.
Marker 2 (below-surface): 2 — connects "currency" framing to broader cellular economics.
Marker 3 (productive uncertainty): 2 — the why-ATP question is structured and answerable.
Marker 4 (real-time development): not applicable in first-post asynchronous; effective 0.
**Total: 6 out of effective 6.** Genuine Y5 signature.

*Contribution B (Student R):*
> "Cellular respiration is a fascinating but complex process that involves multiple interacting pathways. There are many different aspects that students may find challenging. The interplay between glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain offers numerous opportunities for confusion. Each step has its own complexities."

Marker 1: 0 — no specific confusion. The word "challenging" appears without anchor.
Marker 2: 0 — no connection beyond restating what the textbook covers.
Marker 3: 0 — no questions, productive or otherwise.
Marker 4: not applicable; effective 0.
**Total: 0 out of effective 6.** Borrowed-certainty signature.

The two contributions are roughly equal in word count, grammar, and surface fluency. The rubric makes explicit what the instructor felt while reading them.

**Live coding during synchronous discussion.** A clipboard with four tick-boxes per student, one column per marker. Mark each tick as the contribution surfaces it. Total at the end of class. Works for seminars and small-group discussion. Does not scale to a 30-person lecture without a recording.

**The one-minute confusion paper.** End every class with: "Name the most confusing thing about today's material, and be specific about what is confusing to *you*." Collect on the way out. Read for markers. Cheapest possible Y5 instrument. Works in any class size. Does not require live coding.

**The position-change probe.** Mid-discussion, ask: "Has anyone changed their mind about X during this conversation? What changed?" Genuine engagers can name what shifted and why. Borrowed-certainty students either say nothing or produce a generic "I see it differently now." This is the cheapest way to elicit Marker 4 directly.

A practitioner cost note. The rubric takes about 90 seconds per contribution once internalized. Don't try to apply it to every post in every discussion — sample. Five posts per assignment, drawn across the performance range, gives you most of the diagnostic signal. The point is to develop calibrated practitioner judgment, not to grade exhaustively.

A practitioner caution. The rubric is yours, not the student's. It is a teacher tool. Sharing the rubric *with students* turns it into a performance target and produces the trained-performer failure mode the chapter has already warned about. The exception is the explicit metacognitive intervention — telling students "I'm going to start asking about specific confusions because that's how learning gets visible" — which is honest and useful and does not require revealing the rubric itself.

**Practitioner implication:** the rubric is a scaffold for your judgment, not a replacement for it. Your judgment is the instrument.

## What a platform adds

NLP-based classification of discussion contributions — Carolyn Rosé's group at Carnegie Mellon has built systems that automatically score discussion features at scale. Automated Y5 scoring on transcripts. Longitudinal tracking of each student's Y5 trajectory. Identification of students whose contributions cluster near zero across an entire term. For Glimmer-style transcripts (student-with-AI conversation before a human session), the platform can score the student's questions for Y5 markers — the student's questions, not the AI's responses, are where texture lives.

None of this is required to start. The four-marker rubric works on paper.

## Worked example: five discussion posts, one prompt

An instructor in a high school biology class assigns a discussion post at the end of the cellular respiration unit. Prompt: "Pick one thing about cellular respiration that you found confusing, and explain why it was confusing to you specifically."

She receives 28 posts. She picks five at random, prints them, and applies the rubric.

**Post 1 (Student A).** "The chemiosmotic theory was really confusing because Peter Mitchell apparently spent decades on it before anyone believed him, and I read the textbook section three times and I still don't get *why* the proton gradient does work. I get that there's an electrochemical gradient and I get that protons want to flow back through. What I don't get is — if ATP synthase is just a turbine, why couldn't the cell make ATP using any gradient? Why does it have to be protons? I think the answer is something about how the F1 part actually grabs onto ADP and phosphate, but I'm not sure if the gradient is doing chemistry or just physics."

Marker 1: 2. Marker 2: 2 (brings Mitchell history in). Marker 3: 2 (structured question). Marker 4: 0 (async). **Total: 6/6.** Genuine.

**Post 2 (Student B).** "Cellular respiration is a multi-step process involving glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Each step plays an important role in extracting energy from glucose. The complexity of these interconnected pathways can be challenging for students. Multiple factors contribute to the overall efficiency of the process."

Marker 1: 0. Marker 2: 0. Marker 3: 0. Marker 4: 0. **Total: 0/6.** Borrowed certainty.

**Post 3 (Student C).** "What confused me the most was honestly just the number of letters. NAD, NADH, FAD, FADH2, GTP, ATP, ADP — I kept losing track of which one was the high-energy version. By the end of glycolysis I was treating NADH and FADH2 as basically interchangeable, but I think they're not, because the textbook keeps saying NADH yields more ATP. So I'm confused about why two electrons from NADH yield more than two electrons from FADH2 if they end up at the same chain."

Marker 1: 2. Marker 2: 1 (within-unit connection, not outside). Marker 3: 2 (structured comparison question). Marker 4: 0 (async). **Total: 5/6.** Genuine.

**Post 4 (Student D).** "I was confused about a lot of things. The process is complicated and there's a lot to keep track of. I found the diagrams in the book hard to follow. Some parts made more sense than others. I think practice problems would help me understand it better."

Marker 1: 1 (mentions confusion, no anchor). Marker 2: 0. Marker 3: 0. Marker 4: 0. **Total: 1/6.** Borrowed certainty, possibly disengaged.

**Post 5 (Student E).** "I was confused why we need oxygen at the end. The textbook says oxygen is the final electron acceptor, but I wondered why we couldn't just use a different one. Then in chemistry I learned about reduction potentials and I think oxygen is the strongest available acceptor in our environment, which is why aerobic respiration evolved. But I'm not sure if that's the right reason or if I'm conflating two ideas."

Marker 1: 2. Marker 2: 2 (brings chemistry in). Marker 3: 2 (structured, contestable). Marker 4: 0 (async). **Total: 6/6.** Genuine — and notably, this student is doing some of the work the next unit is going to ask of her.

The rubric agrees with what the instructor felt as she read the posts. Posts 1, 3, and 5 read as thinking. Posts 2 and 4 read as not. The rubric makes that recognition shareable — she can show the rubric to her department chair and to the parent of Student B and explain *why* a Post 2 grade is not the same as a Post 1 grade, even if both used full sentences.

She does not punish anyone for Post 2. She writes back: "What specifically did you find confusing about glycolysis? Try to describe one moment where you tried to follow what was happening and lost the thread." She is asking for the texture directly. Often the student can produce it when asked; sometimes they cannot. Either way, the conversation is now possible.

**Practitioner implication:** the worked example is the chapter. Pick five posts. Apply the rubric. Have the conversation.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1 (Apply).** Take five of your students' recent discussion posts or written responses from one assignment. Apply the SKT rubric to each. Record the per-marker score (0, 1, or 2) and a one-sentence justification per marker. Note any context where Marker 4 is not observable (asynchronous first-post, etc.) and adjust the maximum total accordingly. Spend roughly 90 seconds per post on the rubric pass.

**Exercise 2 (Analyze).** Identify three discussion posts from a recent assignment that you suspect were AI-assisted. Apply the SKT rubric to each. Describe which markers are absent and what specifically is missing. Then, for one of the three, write one paragraph of follow-up that *invites* the student to produce the texture you suspect they couldn't — without accusation. The follow-up should ask for something specific (Marker 1), a connection (Marker 2), or a structured question (Marker 3).

**Exercise 3 (Create).** Draft one discussion prompt for an upcoming unit in your class that is designed to *maximize* opportunities for genuine markers to appear. The prompt should structurally invite (a) specific confusions ("name a moment where..."), (b) position changes ("what did you initially think and what changed?"), and (c) productive uncertainty ("what question do you have that the chapter didn't answer?"). Avoid prompts that can be answered by paraphrasing the textbook. Test the prompt on one section and compare the resulting posts to the prompts you used previously.

## What would change my mind

A controlled study showing two trained raters applying the four-marker rubric to the same set of discussion contributions and producing wildly divergent scores would substantially weaken the rubric's value as a practitioner tool. The chapter has been honest that inter-rater reliability has not been established. If it turns out the rubric does not even survive within-school calibration, the recommendation would shift to "trust your own consistent judgment over time" rather than "use this rubric."

A demonstration that current-generation AI tutors can reliably produce Y5-texture contributions — specific anchored confusions, structured questions, in-text position changes that respond to peer arguments — would weaken the diagnostic. The argument the chapter makes is that AI lacks the *attempted-task residue* that produces texture. If AI can simulate the residue convincingly, that argument fails. The chapter's bet is that this is structurally harder for the AI than producing fluent prose; the bet is empirical.

A finding that the markers fail to generalize across cultural traditions of discussion — that what looks like Y5 texture in a US/UK classroom is not what good thinking looks like in, say, a Japanese or Mexican classroom (Hatano's work suggests this is a live concern) — would change how the chapter recommends deploying the rubric. The markers may need to be reconsidered for context.

## Still puzzling

The TIKTOC explicitly flagged Y5 as the most subjective component and asked: *can the SKT rubric be specified at a level a practitioner can use without training?* The chapter's answer is the four-marker, 0–2-per-marker, total-0–8 specification above. The honest answer is: it can be used at the practitioner level *with calibration over time*. The first ten posts will give you scores that drift; by post fifty you will be more consistent. The rubric is a scaffold for judgment that improves with use.

Inter-rater reliability is empirically unknown for this specific rubric. The chapter does not pretend otherwise. In practitioner contexts this is probably fine — the Y5 score is conversation-starter material, not high-stakes grading — but it is a real limit.

Cross-cultural generalizability is a real open question. The markers come from US/UK/Western European sociocultural-psychology traditions. The chapter is honest about this.

AI-generated discussion contribution detection is conceptually clear and empirically thin. The chapter makes the conceptual case (AI lacks personal-encounter residue and real-time development) and does not overstate the controlled evidence.

The Glimmer-style transcript case — student in conversation with an AI tutor, transcript reviewed afterward for Y5 markers in the student's questions — is novel and the markers have not been adapted in detail for that context. Chapter 11 may need to revisit.

A note that should not be lost: Y5 is anchored in a *different research tradition* than the other components. Y3, Y4, and Y6 sit in cognitive psychology. Y5 sits in sociocultural and dialogic-education research — Mercer, Resnick, Scardamalia and Bereiter, Soter, Howe. This separation is part of why Y5 adds *independent* information to the ensemble. Different traditions, different signals, different things they catch.

## The wayback callout: Neil Mercer

Neil Mercer is the Cambridge classroom-dialogue researcher whose three-decade program of work on **exploratory talk** is the empirical foundation of Y5. He distinguished three modes of classroom talk — *disputational* (competitive, no joint reasoning), *cumulative* (uncritical agreement, no challenge), and *exploratory* (genuine engagement with each other's ideas, reasoning made explicit, willingness to change position under good argument). The Howe et al. 2019 study, which gave the field its strongest recent evidence that dialogic moves predict measurable end-of-year learning, was a Mercer-collaborative project. Mercer is less famous than John Hattie among practitioners, but the empirical record on what genuine classroom discussion looks like — and what it does not look like — is largely his. The Y5 chapter owes him not just the markers but the recognition that classroom talk is a measurable cognitive event, not just a delivery mechanism for content.

## Bridge to Chapter 8

Five signals so far. Time on difficulty. Errors that cohere. Transfer that survives clothing change. Confidence that tracks knowledge. Discussion that has texture.

The sixth signal lives in time. A student can ace a quiz today and have nothing left in three weeks. A different student can score the same percentage today and hold most of it six months later. The artifact does not distinguish them on the day of the quiz. The decay curve does.

Chapter 8 is Y6: Retrieval Strength Decay Signature. The chapter Bjork and Bjork named theoretically and Roediger and Karpicke confirmed empirically with one of the cleanest experiments in the learning sciences. The implementation requires no platform and is structurally the cheapest of all seven: embed a few items from previous units in each quiz. The decay curve emerges from data your school already collects.
