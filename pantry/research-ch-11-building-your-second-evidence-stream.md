## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

- **Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998).** "Inside the Black Box: Raising Standards Through Classroom Assessment." *Phi Delta Kappan*, 80(2), 139–148. — The paper that established formative assessment as a high-leverage classroom intervention. Effect sizes 0.4–0.7 across the meta-analysis. *The* citation for "small changes in what you ask students change what you learn about their learning."
- **Wiliam, D. (2011).** *Embedded Formative Assessment.* Solution Tree Press. — Practitioner-facing translation of the 1998 paper. Includes the "traffic light" cards for confidence elicitation — directly relevant to Y4 implementation. Should be the single most-cited practitioner source in Chapter 11.
- **Fullan, M. (2007).** *The New Meaning of Educational Change* (4th ed.). Teachers College Press. — Implementation as the bottleneck, not the idea. "Change is a process, not an event." Should anchor the chapter's "start small, iterate" framing.
- **Hall, G. E., & Hord, S. M. (2014).** *Implementing Change: Patterns, Principles, and Potholes* (4th ed.). Pearson. — The Concerns-Based Adoption Model (CBAM) and Stages of Concern. Practical, evidence-grounded model of why teachers resist or adopt change. Useful frame for the department-chair reader who has to lead colleagues into GLP practice.
- **Tyack, D., & Cuban, L. (1995).** *Tinkering Toward Utopia: A Century of Public School Reform.* Harvard University Press. — Why most school reforms fail. The grammar of schooling resists structural change. Important honest framing for the chapter's institutional-design section.
- **Wolf, D. P. (1989).** "Portfolio assessment: Sampling student work." *Educational Leadership*, 46(7), 35–39. — Foundational on process portfolios as longitudinal evidence.
- **Stiggins, R. J. (2007).** "Assessment for learning: A key to motivation and achievement." *Edge*, 2(2), 1–19. — The practitioner-side argument for formative assessment as learning support, not surveillance.

### Key empirical cases

- **Andrade, H. L. (2010).** "Students as the definitive source of formative assessment: Academic self-assessment and the self-regulation of learning." In *Handbook of Formative Assessment.* Routledge. — Self-assessment / rubric work. Directly applicable to Y4 confidence calibration in K-12. *Note: SUNY Albany; woman; well-positioned for the wayback slot.*
- **Wiliam, D., & Thompson, M. (2008).** "Integrating assessment with learning: What will it take to make it work?" In *The Future of Assessment.* — The "five key strategies" of formative assessment. Two of them (clarifying success criteria; activating students as owners of their own learning) map directly onto Y4 calibration practice.
- **Foster, C. (2018, 2021).** Practitioner work on confidence elicitation in UK mathematics classrooms. K-12 deployment evidence. *Flag: relatively recent; check for replication.*
- **Adesope, O. O., Trevisan, D. A., & Sundararajan, N. (2017).** "Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing." *Review of Educational Research*, 87(3), 659–701. — Practice testing meta-analysis; relevant to Y6 retrieval-decay implementation in routine quizzes.

### Ethics of process observation / learning analytics

- **Selwyn, N. (2019).** "What's the problem with learning analytics?" *Journal of Learning Analytics*, 6(3), 11–19. — Critical perspective. Argues learning analytics often serves institutional efficiency over student interest. Essential for the chapter's ethics section.
- **Slade, S., & Prinsloo, P. (2013).** "Learning analytics: Ethical issues and dilemmas." *American Behavioral Scientist*, 57(10), 1510–1529. — Six principles for ethical learning analytics: student as agent; informed consent; data minimization; transparency; etc. Use as the chapter's ethics scaffolding.
- **Williamson, B. (2017).** *Big Data in Education: The Digital Future of Learning, Policy and Practice.* SAGE. — Book-length argument for caution in education-data infrastructures. Less polemical than Selwyn; more comprehensive.
- **UNESCO (2023).** *Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research.* UNESCO. — Policy-level framework for genAI in education; emphasizes pedagogical purpose and human oversight. Anchor for international applicability.
- **U.S. Department of Education, Office of Educational Technology (2023).** *Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Teaching and Learning.* — US federal-level framework. Emphasizes "AI as a teaching assistant, with teacher in the loop." Practitioner-aligned.

### Real deployment experience (per TIKTOC Open Question 4)

- **Confidence elicitation in K-12:** The "traffic light" / RAG technique (red-amber-green cards) is documented in Wiliam (2011) and widely adopted in UK schools post-2010. Documented deployment, not hypothetical. *This is the strongest single deployment-evidence anchor for Ch 11.*
- **Retrieval practice with embedded decay probes:** Cornell University (Pomerance, Greenberg, Walsh) and Harvard's Bok Center have documented deployments. *Flag: most published evidence is on spaced retrieval per se, not "decay probes as borrowed-certainty diagnostic." The latter is GLP-specific framing.*
- **Process portfolios in K-12 + higher ed:** Documented in Vermont's portfolio assessment program (1990s; widely studied); Alverno College (since 1973, ability-based assessment); Stanford d.school portfolio practice. Long-tail empirical base, but mostly pre-AI.

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled

- Formative assessment, well-designed, produces meaningful learning gains (effect sizes 0.4–0.7; Black & Wiliam 1998 and many replications). The chapter's claim that small assessment changes have outsized impact is well-supported.
- Implementation, not idea-quality, is the dominant determinant of school change outcomes (Fullan 2007; Hall & Hord 2014). "Start small, iterate, build coalition" is field-standard implementation advice.
- Confidence elicitation is implementable at near-zero cost in any classroom and produces useful diagnostic information (Wiliam 2011; Adesope et al. 2017).
- Process portfolios produce assessment information that artifact-only assessment misses (Wolf 1989; portfolio-assessment literature).

### What is disputed

- Whether process observation can be cleanly separated from surveillance. Selwyn 2019 argues no — even well-intentioned process data shifts power toward institutions. Wiliam and Stiggins argue yes — purpose and data-ownership determine the distinction. *Live debate; the book's position is that the distinction is real but requires deliberate design choices.*
- How much ethics framing should appear in practitioner-facing AI-in-education materials. UNESCO and US ED Tech reports include substantial ethics framing; some practitioner critics argue this swamps actionable content. The book's stated balance (10–15% of Ch 11) is defensible and consistent with practitioner-focused publications like Wiliam (2011).
- Whether AI-in-education ethics frameworks are too generic to guide specific practitioner decisions. Most current frameworks (UNESCO 2023; US ED 2023) read more as principles than as decision procedures.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)

- AI-in-education has shifted ethics debates from "data privacy" (the 2015–2022 focus) to "process observation legitimacy" (post-2023 focus). The GLP framework sits squarely in the new debate.
- More school districts have implemented at least one component-style process assessment (confidence-elicitation cards being the most common), creating a slowly-growing deployment base.
- AI-detection-tool fatigue has increased educator receptivity to alternative frameworks. The market for what Ch 11 offers is bigger in 2026 than in 2023.

## 3. Application Domain Examples

- **K-12 (primary reader):** A science department chair adds two items to existing quizzes: (1) a confidence-elicitation question after each item (Y4) and (2) one "decay probe" question from a unit two-units prior (Y6). Zero infrastructure cost. Documented in Wiliam (2011) for Y4; novel for Y6 as borrowed-certainty diagnostic.
- **Higher education:** A first-year writing program adds one near-transfer and one far-transfer prompt per unit (Y3) plus a 100-word "confusion note" with each major submission (Y5-lite). Implementable with no LMS changes.
- **Clinical education:** Bedside-teaching log captures hint-response sequence (Y7) for each learner. Faculty member records "stuck-after-partial, moved-after-full" or "moved-after-partial." Aggregated across rotation, the data informs feedback. Documented in some residency programs as part of milestone assessment.
- **Community college (worked example in TIKTOC):** Y4 confidence + Y5 confusion note. Faculty member uses profiles to prioritize outreach, not grade changes. This is the model deployment for the chapter.

### Real deployment status check (per Open Question 4)

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection

Chapter 11 is the book's payoff. Two thesis-connections:

1. **Process-based measurement is not a platform, it is a practice.** The chapter must close the gap between framework and behavior change. The reader who closes the book without picking two components has not been served by the book regardless of how well Chapters 1–10 landed. The chapter's structure (triage → starting point by context → first-term plan) is designed to force a concrete commitment.
2. **The second evidence stream is additive, not replacement.** The thesis claim is not that artifact assessment dies; it is that genuine learning requires a *second* evidence stream alongside the artifact. The chapter must explicitly resist the "tear it all down" reading. Practitioners are correctly suspicious of frameworks that ask them to abandon current practice. GLP asks them to add, not replace.

The ethics section connects the thesis to the social-license question: the framework is defensible because it serves the student's learning, not the institution's surveillance interest. This distinction is structural to the GLP claim that process observation is legitimate when purpose and data-ownership align with the learner's interest.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research

The chapter's instructional design must mirror its content: model the start-small-iterate principle in the chapter itself. Five delivery moves:

1. **The triage framework first.** Open with the component-accessibility ladder. The reader needs to know which two components fit their context before they receive instructions.
2. **Starting points by context, with named example.** The K-12 / higher-ed / clinical-education branching keeps the chapter from feeling generic. Each branch must have a specific named example (community-college instructor, secondary-science chair, etc.). This is where TIKTOC Open Question 4 lands: the examples must be grounded enough to be believable, even if some are synthetic composites.
3. **The first-term plan as scaffolded exercise.** Exercise 1 ("identify the two GLP components you will begin observing this term") is the chapter's most important pedagogical artifact. Should be the longest of the three exercises, with a structured response template.
4. **Ethics in the middle, not at the end.** Placing ethics last risks reading as compliance afterthought. Placing it in the middle (after starting points, before institutional design) signals that ethical design is a *deployment* concern, not an obligation.
5. **The closing as a single action.** The TIKTOC's closing — "start with one question: how confident are you in your answer? — track the gap" — is the right ending. The reader leaves with one concrete action. Do not dilute it.

## 7. Representation and Display Research

Four artifacts earn their place.

1. **The component-accessibility ladder.** Seven rows (Y1–Y7), three columns (infrastructure required, time-to-first-data, interpretive complexity). Reader can identify their two-component starting point in under three minutes.
2. **The starting-point flowchart.** Decision tree: "Do you teach K-12 / higher ed / clinical?" → component recommendations. Decision-tree format makes the chapter actively useful, not just informative.
3. **The first-term timeline.** Week-by-week schedule for a 14-week term showing when data collection begins, when first-look-at-the-data happens, when conversations with students start. Concrete, calendarized, low-overhead.
4. **The "observation for learning vs. observation for surveillance" matrix.** Two-by-two: purpose × data ownership. Quadrants are labeled. Practitioners locate their planned implementation in one quadrant.

Tables: a "two-sentence explanation of friction traces" template (TIKTOC Exercise 2) and three or four worked example explanations from different practitioner perspectives.

Avoid: data-dashboard mockups, platform-screen UI mockups, anything that suggests the reader needs a platform.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps

1. **(Per TIKTOC Open Question 4 — flagged explicitly.)** The chapter's starting-points-by-context recommendations require real deployment experience, not hypothetical claims. The Y4 and Y5 starting points are well-grounded (Wiliam, Cross/Angelo); the Y6 decay-probe and Y3 transfer-pair starting points are less well-documented as routine practitioner workflows. *Gap: a structured field study of practitioners who have implemented one or two GLP components for at least a term would substantially strengthen this chapter. The book should commission or compile such cases before final draft.*
2. **(Per TIKTOC Open Question 5 — flagged explicitly.)** The chapter's ethics balance is currently planned at ~10–15% of the chapter. The research argues this is the correct proportion for a practitioner book — enough to establish the legitimacy distinction between learning observation and surveillance, not so much that practical content is swamped. UNESCO 2023 and US ED 2023 frame this consensus.
3. **Subgroup equity of starting-point recommendations.** Y4 confidence elicitation may underestimate performance in students with metacognitive deficits or anxiety; Y5 confusion notes may underestimate ELL students. The chapter should acknowledge these without offering false confidence in mitigation.
4. **Institutional design implications.** The chapter touches on longitudinal process documentation as primary evidence. This is a much larger argument than the chapter can carry. The book should either flag it as a future-volume question or treat it briefly with a clear cross-reference to the GLP preprint.
5. **Adoption-curve evidence.** No empirical data exists on whether department-chair-led GLP adoption succeeds or fails in K-12. The Hall & Hord CBAM framework predicts the dynamics; actual results would require longitudinal study.
6. **AI-policy alignment.** Many districts and universities are issuing AI policies. Chapter 11 should anticipate the reader who wants to know how GLP relates to existing institutional AI policy. The honest answer: GLP is compatible with most policies because it sidesteps the detection question, but local policy review is necessary.
