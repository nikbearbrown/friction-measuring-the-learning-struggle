# Research: Chapter 05 — Y3: Cross-Context Transfer
## Friction Traces: Measuring the Struggle That Proves the Learning

**Chapter one-line:** Genuine understanding transfers to new surface features. Borrowed certainty pattern-matches to the original context and fails when surface features change. The transfer gap is the signal.
**Research date:** 2026-05-27

---

## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

- **Thorndike, E. L., & Woodworth, R. S. (1901). "The Influence of Improvement in One Mental Function upon the Efficiency of Other Functions." *Psychological Review* 8: 247–261, 384–395, 553–564.** The founding three-part series. Identical-elements theory: transfer occurs when the new task shares specific cognitive components with the trained task. The pessimistic foundation against which all subsequent transfer optimism is measured.
- **Barnett, S. M., & Ceci, S. J. (2002). "When and Where Do We Apply What We Learn? A Taxonomy for Far Transfer." *Psychological Bulletin* 128(4): 612–637.** The definitive contemporary taxonomy. Nine dimensions on which transfer can be near or far: knowledge domain, physical context, temporal context, functional context, social context, modality, plus the content dimensions of skill and performance change. Y3 is fundamentally an operationalization of Barnett & Ceci's dimensions.
- **Detterman, D. K., & Sternberg, R. J. (Eds.). (1993). *Transfer on Trial: Intelligence, Cognition, and Instruction.* Ablex.** The "transfer is rare" position stated forcefully. Detterman's chapter argues that demonstrated transfer in lab studies is the exception, not the rule. This is the discomfort the chapter has to sit with: real transfer is hard, which is exactly why it's diagnostic.
- **Bransford, J. D., & Schwartz, D. L. (1999). "Rethinking Transfer: A Simple Proposal With Multiple Implications." *Review of Research in Education* 24: 61–100.** The most-cited modern reframing. Argues that "direct application" tests of transfer underestimate it; what matters is *preparation for future learning* (PFL). A student who has genuinely learned can pick up the new context faster, even if they can't directly apply yesterday's lesson. Practitioner-friendly because it shifts the question from "did transfer happen?" to "is the learner now better positioned to learn the new thing?"
- **Salomon, G., & Perkins, D. N. (1989). "Rocky Roads to Transfer: Rethinking Mechanisms of a Neglected Phenomenon." *Educational Psychologist* 24(2): 113–142.** The "high road / low road" distinction. *Low road* transfer = automatic, reflexive, comes from extensive practice with surface features. *High road* transfer = effortful, mindful abstraction of principles. Y3 is structurally a high-road transfer probe — surface features change, so reflexive pattern-matching fails.

### Key empirical cases

- **Morris, C. D., Bransford, J. D., & Franks, J. J. (1977). "Levels of Processing versus Transfer Appropriate Processing." *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 16(5): 519–533.** The empirical correction to "deeper processing is always better." Transfer depends on the *match* between encoding and retrieval conditions. If you study sound, you remember sounds. If you study meaning, you remember meaning. Practical implication for Y3: a transfer item must legitimately test the same underlying processing, just in different surface clothing.
- **Chase, W. G., & Simon, H. A. (1973). "Perception in Chess." *Cognitive Psychology* 4(1): 55–81.** Chess masters reconstruct meaningful positions accurately and random positions no better than novices. Schema is what transfers; pattern-matching to surface features is not the same skill. The reference case for "expertise is schema, not surface familiarity."
- **Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., & Glaser, R. (1981). "Categorization and Representation of Physics Problems by Experts and Novices." *Cognitive Science* 5(2): 121–152.** Novices sort physics problems by surface features (inclined planes, springs). Experts sort by deep principle (conservation of energy, Newton's second law). The classic empirical demonstration that *what features you notice* is the diagnostic — exactly the Y3 question.
- **Gick, M. L., & Holyoak, K. J. (1980). "Analogical Problem Solving." *Cognitive Psychology* 12(3): 306–355.** The "Duncker radiation problem" with the general-and-fortress analog. Spontaneous transfer rates without a hint were near zero; with a hint to use the prior story, rates jumped to ~75%. The empirical bedrock for "transfer is rare without prompting" and the case study for how schema abstraction can be probed in the lab.
- **Hatano, G., & Inagaki, K. (1986). "Two Courses of Expertise." In H. Stevenson, H. Azuma, & K. Hakuta (Eds.), *Child Development and Education in Japan.* Freeman.** The routine-expertise / adaptive-expertise distinction. Routine experts get faster and more accurate on familiar problems. Adaptive experts can extend, modify, and invent for new situations. Y3 measures adaptive expertise. Routine expertise is what borrowed certainty produces.

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled

Surface-feature pattern-matching is not transfer. Chi et al. (1981) and Chase & Simon (1973) are textbook material. Experts and novices differ in *what they notice*, not just in *how much they know*. This is consensus across cognitive psychology and education research for more than four decades.

Schemas are real and abstract from surface features. Sweller's cognitive load theory and the broader schema literature converge: durable expertise involves a hierarchical schema that abstracts away from the specific instance. The schema is what permits recognition of "this is a Newton's-third-law problem" when the problem mentions an astronaut, not a hockey puck.

Far transfer is hard. Detterman's strong claim — "transfer is rare" — overstates it, but the consensus position is that *spontaneous* far transfer (no hint, no prompt) is genuinely uncommon. Most successful transfer in school settings is prompted near-transfer.

Transfer-appropriate processing (Morris et al. 1977) is settled: encoding-retrieval match matters more than abstract "depth." This is why a well-designed transfer item must preserve the underlying processing demand and only vary surface features.

### What is disputed

Whether far transfer can be reliably *taught* through general training (general critical thinking, general problem solving, "21st-century skills") is disputed. Detterman and Sternberg's edited volume took the strong negative position. The general-skills training literature has continued to produce mixed results. For practitioners, the implication is to teach for transfer *within a domain*, with explicit variation of surface features.

Whether Bransford & Schwartz's "preparation for future learning" lens fully resolves the "transfer is rare" finding is contested. Critics argue PFL relaxes the criterion so much that it's hard to falsify. Defenders note that direct-application tests systematically underestimate what students have learned.

Whether AI assistance produces *any* far-transfer signature is currently an open empirical question. Most published RCTs (Bastani 2025, Kestin 2025) focus on immediate post-test performance, not far transfer. The conceptual prediction is clear — borrowed certainty pattern-matches to the AI's context and should fail when surface features change — but the empirical record is thin.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)

Schema-based instruction has been re-examined under the lens of generative AI. If an AI can produce a fluent answer to the textbook problem but the student doesn't recognize the principle in a novel scenario, the AI has not transferred learning; it has substituted output. This makes far-transfer probes more diagnostic, not less, in the AI era.

Variation theory (Marton et al.) has gained traction in math education as a method to *engineer* transfer by systematically varying which features change between problems. This gives practitioners a concrete design move: hold the principle constant, vary the surface.

Several recent reviews note that the "near vs. far" distinction is itself a continuum, not a dichotomy. Practitioner translation: design transfer items at multiple distances, not just two.

## 3. Application Domain Examples

**High school physics (primary reader's home turf).** Newton's third law. Near transfer item: a hockey puck struck by a stick (textbook image). Far transfer item: an astronaut pushing off the wall of the International Space Station. Same principle, completely different surface features. Students who scored well on the near item and poorly on the far item are showing exactly the Y3 borrowed-certainty signature.

**High school biology.** Natural selection. Near transfer: peppered moths in industrial England (canonical case). Far transfer: antibiotic resistance in hospital-acquired infections. The mechanism is identical; the timescale and ecology differ. Students whose explanations rely on the moth scenario as scaffolding rather than the underlying selection mechanism will show the gap.

**High school chemistry.** Le Chatelier's principle. Near transfer: a sealed flask with a colored gas equilibrium. Far transfer: ocean acidification as the carbonate equilibrium shifts under rising CO₂. Same principle, completely different scale and stakes.

**Higher ed introductory statistics.** Sampling distributions and standard error. Near transfer: estimating a population mean from a sample (textbook example with the same n and same distribution shape). Far transfer: interpreting a political poll's margin of error during election season. Pattern-matchers handle the textbook problem fine; only students with schema can adapt.

**Clinical education (medical).** Beta blockade (the TIKTOC opening case). Near transfer: a board-style question that names "beta-1 selective antagonist" and asks for indications. Far transfer: a patient presentation with no drug-class language, where the student must recognize that the scenario calls for the principle. This is exactly the gap that frustrates clinical educators: students who passed pharm can't see the application at the bedside.

**Instructional design (secondary reader).** Designing transfer-appropriate assessment is itself a design problem. The chapter can model it: hold the cognitive demand constant, vary the cover story, the modality, and the social context.

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection

Y3 sits at the intersection of the book's two strongest arguments. First, the decoupling argument: AI produces a fluent answer to the *given problem* and does so by extensively pattern-matching to similar problems in its training distribution. The AI's output is, almost by construction, surface-feature transfer. When the student offloads the problem to the AI and absorbs the answer, the student inherits surface-feature transfer too — they can recognize the *kind* of problem the AI just solved, but they have not built the schema that would let them recognize the principle when the surface features shift.

Second, the seven-signal architecture: Y3 is one of the most theoretically grounded components because schema-formation has 50+ years of cognitive psychology behind it (Chase & Simon, Chi et al., Gick & Holyoak, Bransford & Schwartz). The mechanistic chain is clean: genuine engagement → schema formation → schema-based recognition of the principle under varied surface features → near-transfer and far-transfer performance both elevated, small transfer gap.

The Y3 signature is structurally hard to fake. To fake far-transfer performance, a student would need to *know which features are surface and which are deep* — and that knowledge is exactly the schema we're trying to detect. This is the cleanest case in the seven-signal framework of "the fake requires the real." This connects directly to Chapter 10's gaming-cost argument.

The chapter must be honest about one limit: Y3 doesn't distinguish "AI-assisted learner who built the schema anyway" from "unassisted learner who built the schema." The signal is *did the schema form*, not *how*. That's a feature, not a bug — the framework's question is "did you learn?" not "did you use AI?"

## 5. The AI Wayback Machine — Candidate Figures

**Mary L. Gick (Carleton University, Canada).** Co-author with Keith Holyoak of the foundational analogical transfer studies (1980, 1983). Less famous than Holyoak but the empirical work bears her first authorship. Woman, accessible historical figure, primary archive material available through Carleton. Recommended for the chapter's "wayback" figure — she does double duty as the empirical scientist behind the iconic Duncker radiation problem studies, and as a corrective to the Anglo-male-Stanford tilt of the transfer literature.

**Giyoo Hatano (1935–2006, University of Tokyo, then Keio).** Co-developer with Kayoko Inagaki of the routine-expertise / adaptive-expertise distinction. Japanese cultural psychology of learning. Non-Western perspective on transfer that doesn't reduce to Anglo-American cognitive psychology. Strong "lesser-known + non-Western" candidate for the chapter wayback. The routine-vs-adaptive frame is also pedagogically clean: it gives the practitioner reader two named patterns that map directly onto borrowed certainty vs. genuine learning.

**Susan R. Goldman (University of Illinois Chicago).** Transfer in problem-based learning, especially in disciplinary literacy. Woman, contemporary, accessible. Useful if the chapter wants a higher-ed-leaning figure rather than a historic one.

**Recommended pick for this book:** Hatano. The non-Western diversity counts toward the cross-chapter goal; the routine/adaptive distinction is the single best plain-language frame for what Y3 measures; the practitioner reader gets a memorable named concept ("are your students routine experts or adaptive experts?") that survives the chapter.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research

The chapter must deliver three moves the practitioner can use the next morning.

**Move 1: Pairs, not singletons.** Don't ask "did the student transfer?" Ask "what's the gap between near and far?" The pair is diagnostic in a way no single item can be. This is a Cepeda-style design principle: the comparison carries the signal.

**Move 2: Vary surface, hold principle.** The practitioner has to design transfer items, not just grade them. The chapter should give a 3-step recipe: (a) state the underlying principle in one sentence, (b) write a textbook-style near-item, (c) rewrite the same principle into a scenario from a completely different domain. This is variation-theory pedagogy in two paragraphs.

**Move 3: Don't penalize the slow far-transfer.** Bransford & Schwartz's PFL frame matters here. A student who needs a hint to see the connection is not failing — they may be learning *during* the assessment. The chapter should give the reader permission to score Y3 as a continuum, not a pass/fail.

The chapter delivery should follow the same Feynman-style arc the book uses throughout: open with the beta blockade case (medical, vivid, transferable), unfold what schema means (Chi et al., physics-problem sorting study, retold in plain language), name the trade-off (far transfer is rare and hard to teach, but it's diagnostic exactly because it's hard), and hand the reader the two-item near/far recipe to use next week.

## 7. Representation and Display Research

How to observe Y3 without a platform — the practitioner-without-Medhavy section.

**Method 1: The paired-item quiz.** For each major concept in the unit, write one near-transfer item and one far-transfer item. Score them separately. Compute the gap (near% – far%) at the student level. Students whose gap is small show genuine Y3. Students with a gap larger than ~20 percentage points show borrowed certainty's signature. This is the simplest, cheapest, lowest-tech implementation.

**Method 2: The cold-call recognition probe.** After teaching a concept, present a scenario that uses the concept *without naming it.* Students must recognize that the concept applies. This is exactly the beta blockade clinical-vignette pattern. It works in any domain where principles can be embedded in scenarios.

**Method 3: The sort task.** Borrow Chi et al. directly. Print 12 problems on cards. Ask students to sort them into groups and label each group. Surface-feature sorters reveal themselves immediately (they group by cover story); schema-based sorters group by principle. This works in 15 minutes of class time, requires nothing more than paper, and gives the instructor a qualitative Y3 read on every student simultaneously.

**Method 4: The cross-discipline retrieval prompt.** Ask students at the start of a new unit: "Where else have you seen something like this?" Genuine schema-holders surface connections the textbook didn't make. Borrowed-certainty students name the previous chapter's example and stop. This is also a Bransford & Schwartz PFL probe — it captures preparation-for-future-learning without an exam.

**What a platform adds:** automated transfer-item generation across surface variations, large-N transfer-gap distributions to calibrate "what counts as a large gap" empirically, longitudinal tracking of the gap across the term. None of this is necessary to *start* observing Y3.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps

**The gap-size threshold is empirically open.** What near–far percentage-point gap counts as the borrowed-certainty signature? The conceptual claim is "near high, far low," but the cutoff is a calibration problem the field has not solved at the practitioner-actionable level. The chapter should be honest: pick a threshold that fits your context, refine it with experience.

**AI's effect on far-transfer performance is underspecified in published RCTs.** Bastani 2025 measured exam performance, not specifically far-transfer items varied along Barnett & Ceci dimensions. The prediction is clear; the controlled empirical record is thin. Flag for verify.

**Whether teaching for transfer (variation theory, contrast cases) prevents the borrowed-certainty Y3 signature is an open instructional design question.** It is plausible that good transfer-pedagogy makes the signal less diagnostic because more students reach genuine schema. This is a feature, not a bug — but it changes what the signal *means* across classrooms.

**The "preparation for future learning" version of Y3 has no clean classroom operationalization.** The PFL framing is theoretically attractive but practically harder to score than direct-application transfer. The chapter should mention it without making it the main move.

**GLP / Frictional / seven-signal flag.** The integration of Y3 with six other signals into a composite GLP score is a proprietary Humanitarians AI framework. The component literature (transfer, schema) is durable and uncontroversial; the composite is the bet.
