# Research: Chapter 04 — Y2: Error Trajectory Coherence
## Friction Traces: Measuring the Struggle That Proves the Learning

**Chapter one-line:** Genuine misconceptions produce coherent errors that evolve predictably as the mental model develops. Random errors produce no coherent pattern. The coherence is the signal.
**Research date:** 2026-05-27

---

## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

- **Larkin, J. H., McDermott, J., Simon, D. P., & Simon, H. A. (1980). Expert and novice performance in solving physics problems. *Science*, 208(4450), 1335–1342.** The foundational expert-novice paper. Novices reason from surface features (an inclined plane is "about angles"); experts reason from deep principles (it is "about conservation"). Novice *errors* are predictable from their surface-feature reasoning. The first systematic demonstration that error patterns are diagnostic of underlying mental models.
- **Brown, J. S., & Burton, R. R. (1978). Diagnostic models for procedural bugs in basic mathematical skills. *Cognitive Science*, 2(2), 155–192.** The "BUGGY" system — a generative model of children's subtraction errors based on identifiable procedural "bugs." Demonstrates that errors are not random; they follow coherent patterns generated by specific mis-procedures. The clearest empirical demonstration of error trajectory coherence as a diagnostic. The perfect anchor for Y2.
- **Smith, J. P., diSessa, A. A., & Roschelle, J. (1993). Misconceptions reconceived: A constructivist analysis of knowledge in transition. *The Journal of the Learning Sciences*, 3(2), 115–163.** Knowledge-in-pieces framework — misconceptions are not monolithic; they are *p-prims* (phenomenological primitives) loosely organized. The reframing matters for Y2: error evolution should be understood as the *reorganization* of p-prims, not the deletion of a wrong belief.
- **Chi, M. T. H. (2005). Commonsense conceptions of emergent processes: Why some misconceptions are robust. *The Journal of the Learning Sciences*, 14(2), 161–199.** Some misconceptions are deeply ontological (treating emergent processes as direct causal ones) and persist despite instruction. The chapter must distinguish *surface* misconceptions (easily updated, visible in coherent error trajectories) from *ontological* misconceptions (resistant; Y2 may flatten before genuine change occurs).
- **Treagust, D. F. (1988). Development and use of diagnostic tests to evaluate students' misconceptions in science. *International Journal of Science Education*, 10(2), 159–169.** Two-tier diagnostic tests — answer + reasoning. Practitioner-feasible misconception identification.
- **Driver, R., Squires, A., Rushworth, P., & Wood-Robinson, V. (1994). *Making sense of secondary science: Research into children's ideas*. Routledge.** The canonical misconception catalog. Practitioner-accessible.
- **Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997).** Returns from Ch 2. The mechanism behind Y2.
- **Niv, Y., & Schoenbaum, G. (2008).** Returns from Ch 2.
- **Brown, A. L., & Burton, R. R.** — see Brown & Burton 1978 above; Ann L. Brown is a different person (see Wayback Machine candidates).
- **Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Inside the black box: Raising standards through classroom assessment. *Phi Delta Kappan*, 80(2), 139–148.** Formative assessment foundational paper. Establishes error analysis as central to formative practice.
- **Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research*, 77(1), 81–112.** Feedback specifically responsive to error patterns is one of the most effective educational interventions.

### Key empirical cases
- Brown & Burton 1978 BUGGY (above) — the empirical anchor.
- diSessa's p-prim case studies on intuitive physics — coherent error patterns in students learning Newtonian mechanics.
- VanLehn's research on intelligent tutoring systems (Andes, Atlas) — error-driven scaffolding generates measurable learning gains, indirectly validating Y2's premise.

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled
- **Student errors are non-random and diagnostic.** Brown & Burton, diSessa, Chi — converged finding across decades and domains. Errors are generated by mental models; the mental model can be inferred from the error.
- **Prediction error drives learning.** Schultz-Dayan-Montague at the neural level; behavioral learning rates at the psychological level. Errors are not bugs in learning; they are the *engine* of learning.
- **Coherent error patterns predict learning gains better than overall error rates.** A student making *coherent* errors is closer to a model update than a student making *scattered* errors at the same total rate. Hattie & Timperley's feedback literature converges on this.
- **Misconception catalogs exist for major K-12 content areas** (Driver et al.; the AAAS *Atlas of Science Literacy*). The infrastructure for misconception-aware error coding is in place.

### What is disputed
- **Monolithic misconception vs. knowledge-in-pieces.** Whether students hold *unified wrong beliefs* (Posner/Strike conceptual change tradition) or *fragmented p-prims* (diSessa tradition). Has consequences for what coherent error evolution should look like.
- **The boundary between productive and unproductive error patterns.** Some error trajectories spiral toward correct understanding; others spiral toward more entrenched misconception. Identifying which is which in real time is hard.
- **Whether automated misconception coding (LLM-based, e.g.) is reliable enough for high-stakes use.** Recent work (2024–2025) on LLM-coded student responses shows promise but limited validation.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)
- **LLM-based error coding tools** can now classify open-response errors against misconception taxonomies with reasonable accuracy for many domains. This makes Y2 computationally tractable in ways it was not five years ago.
- **The Bastani 2025 finding** raises an empirically open question about AI-assisted error patterns: do AI-assisted students produce *coherent* errors at all? Anecdotal reports suggest *scattered* errors — the AI is correct in one place and hallucinates in another, producing patterns inconsistent with any developmental misconception. The framework's empirical bet.
- **Knowledge-in-pieces has converged with cognitive science** of learning more broadly; the p-prim language is now mainstream in math and science education research.

## 3. Application Domain Examples

1. **Elementary subtraction — the BUGGY domain.** Brown & Burton's original case. The "smaller-from-larger" bug (in 25 − 17, child computes 7 − 5 = 2 in the units column) is generative; it predicts the same error structure across many problems. *The* clearest demonstration of error trajectory coherence.
2. **Middle school fractions.** Numerator-denominator confusion; operation-on-numerator-only error; sign errors. The TIKTOC worked example. A misconception map exists; the teacher codes student errors by category and watches the trajectory.
3. **High school physics — Newtonian mechanics.** The "force-as-motion" misconception (objects move *because* of force, including impetus-like persistence) generates predictable errors across kinematics, dynamics, and circular motion problems. diSessa's domain.
4. **High school chemistry — limiting reagent.** Students mistake "more of reactant A" for "produces more product" without checking stoichiometric ratios. Coherent errors across many problems; the trajectory updates when the student internalizes the ratio constraint.
5. **Medical education — drug-dosage calculations.** Students who err on dimensional analysis err *consistently* (e.g., always inverting the conversion factor). The error trajectory across a problem set tells the instructor what to teach.

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection

Y2 is *the* signal where the chapter must make the **prediction-error mechanism explicit** — because Y2 is the most directly mechanistic of the seven signals. The argument structure:

1. **The neurobiological prediction.** Each genuine error is a *prediction error* in Schultz-Dayan-Montague terms — a gap between the student's predicted outcome and the actual outcome. The dopaminergic signal that this gap produces drives schema update.
2. **Schema updates are *cumulative*.** A student does not jump from misconception A to correct understanding; she moves to misconception A′ (closer), then A′′ (closer still), then to the target. Each step is small. The error pattern *across* steps is the trajectory.
3. **The genuine signature.** Errors cluster around a current misconception. The error type *changes* at the update event (the moment the dopaminergic signal triggers a substantial model revision). Subsequent performance reflects the *new* (slightly better) model.
4. **The borrowed certainty signature.** The AI is not building any model. When it errs, it errs in ways that do not reflect a developing mental model — hallucinations, off-task responses, internally inconsistent claims. A student whose work shows AI errors will show *scattered* error types: a sophisticated correct answer here, a wildly off-target error there, no developmental pattern across items.
5. **Independence from artifact quality and from Y1.** A student can produce a polished artifact (artifact quality says competent), can spend appropriate time on hard items (Y1 looks fine — easy to fake by knowing which items are hard), and still show scattered errors because the AI assistance is *piecewise*. Y2 is independent of both.
6. **Why Y2 is hard to fake.** Faking Y2 requires *knowing the misconception space* — which incorrect answers are conceptually adjacent. A student trying to fake genuine error trajectory would need to know which wrong answers to produce. This is itself a piece of the learning the student is trying to fake. The ensemble argument for Ch 10 develops here.

The chapter must demonstrate that Y2 is *legible to a teacher* even without computational tooling. The simple misconception-coding spreadsheet in the worked example is the bridge.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research

The reader needs:

1. **A working definition of "error trajectory" she can hold without arithmetic.** "If errors cluster around the same wrong idea and then move to a different wrong idea before the student gets it right, that's a trajectory. If errors are scattered without pattern, that's not." Concrete, recognizable.
2. **A misconception map for her domain.** The chapter cannot supply maps for every domain. It should *point* to existing catalogs — Driver et al. for K-12 science; the AAAS Atlas; subject-area journals — and demonstrate how to *use* a map even when she has to build her own.
3. **A coding spreadsheet template.** Five columns: student, item, error category, date, notes. The simplest possible artifact. Used across five assessments produces a visible trajectory.
4. **Permission to act on imperfect coding.** Misconception coding is interpretive. The chapter should be direct: imperfect coding done consistently across the term is far more informative than waiting for a perfect taxonomy.

Tone rule: the chapter is *not* a misconception research review. It is a working tool. Brown & Burton appears as the *reason* the pattern exists; the misconception catalogs appear as resources; the bulk of the chapter is operational.

## 7. Representation and Display Research

**Y1–Y7 chapters require a "How to observe without a platform" section.** For Y2:

**Low-cost classroom observation methods for Y2:**

- **The misconception-coding spreadsheet.** Five columns above. The instructor codes each wrong answer by misconception type from a domain-specific list (5–8 categories usually). Across five assessments per student, the trajectory becomes visible.
- **Two-tier diagnostic tests (Treagust 1988).** Item: answer + reasoning. The reasoning lets the instructor see *which* misconception generated the wrong answer. Practitioner-feasible in any domain; existing item banks for many.
- **Error journals.** Students keep a running log of their own errors and what they think generated each one. Self-coded misconception trajectories; useful both as data and as metacognitive intervention (links to Y4).
- **Exit-ticket misconception probes.** One question at the end of class, designed to elicit one specific misconception if it persists. Track the proportion of students showing it across weeks; the trajectory at the class level is visible.
- **Comparing error patterns across closed-book and open-book/take-home contexts.** A student whose closed-book errors are coherent (clusters around a developing misconception) but whose take-home errors are scattered shows a Y2 signature *only* in the closed-book context — the signature of having genuinely engaged when she did engage. The contrast is the diagnostic.

**What a platform adds:** automated coding against a misconception adjacency matrix; Y2 score per student per concept; identification of stuck misconceptions across the cohort; early-warning when a student's trajectory is *not* moving toward target.

**Suggested figures:**
- **The trajectory plot.** X-axis: assessment number. Y-axis: misconception category. One line per student. Genuine trajectories *move* across categories. Borrowed-certainty trajectories are flat or random.
- **The misconception space diagram.** A network of conceptually adjacent misconceptions for one specific domain (e.g., fraction operations). Adjacent nodes connected by edges. The genuine learner's trajectory traces edges; the borrowed-certainty student's pattern is non-graph-like.
- **The coding spreadsheet template.** Show the template the reader can copy. Implementation, not theory.

Avoid: confusion matrices and inter-rater reliability tables (the research-paper voice).

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps

- **Conceptual adjacency / misconception-space mapping outside of mathematics and Newtonian mechanics is sparse.** Driver et al. covers K-12 science; AAAS Atlas exists; but for many domains (history, literature, second-language acquisition, clinical reasoning), formal misconception maps are limited. The chapter should be honest about this and offer the practitioner-build option.
- **No published direct evidence on AI-assisted error pattern characteristics.** Whether AI-assisted student work produces scattered (non-coherent) errors as the framework predicts is the empirical bet. Bastani 2025 may have item-level error data; flag for reanalysis.
- **The boundary between Y2 and Y4 (calibration).** A student whose errors trajectory moves correctly *and* whose confidence tracks her growing competence is showing genuine learning on both. The signals are partially correlated; the chapter should note this and defer the independence argument to Ch 10.
- **LLM-based error coding validity.** Promising, not yet rigorously validated for misconception-typing in most domains. Practitioner reader should not be told this is solved.
