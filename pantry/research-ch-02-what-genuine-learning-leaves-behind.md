## 1. Primary Sources

### Foundational papers and texts

- **Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. *Science*, 275(5306), 1593–1599.** The temporal-difference learning paper — dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area fire on *unexpected* reward and on cues that *predict* reward, and reduce firing when expected reward is omitted. The mathematical structure of error-driven learning at the cellular level. Foundational for Y2 and Y6.
- **Niv, Y., & Schoenbaum, G. (2008). Dialogues on prediction errors. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 12(7), 265–272.** Accessible bridge between cellular dopamine work and behavioral learning. Useful for practitioner translation.
- **Cotman, C. W., & Berchtold, N. C. (2002). Exercise: a behavioral intervention to enhance brain health and plasticity. *Trends in Neurosciences*, 25(6), 295–301.** BDNF upregulation under aerobic challenge — *not* directly cognitive challenge. **Caution:** the book should *not* claim "thinking hard upregulates BDNF" without the chain explicitly drawn. The cleaner claim is that BDNF mediates plasticity and is responsive to *effortful demand*.
- **Yang, G., Pan, F., & Gan, W. B. (2009). Stably maintained dendritic spines are associated with lifelong memories. *Nature*, 462(7275), 920–924.** Motor learning in mice produced rapid spine formation; spines that persisted predicted memory persistence. **Caution: mouse motor cortex, not human declarative learning.** Generalization is mechanistic, not direct. Use as "the mechanism that *underlies* memory consolidation" not "the proof that essays produce spines."
- **Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2011). Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way: Creating desirable difficulties to enhance learning.** In M. A. Gernsbacher et al. (Eds.), *Psychology and the real world: Essays illustrating fundamental contributions to society* (pp. 56–64). Worth.
- **Soderstrom, N. C., & Bjork, R. A. (2015). Learning versus performance: An integrative review. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 10(2), 176–199.** Returns from Ch 1. The conceptual core of the chapter.
- **Bjork, R. A. (1975). Retrieval as a memory modifier.** And Bjork's "New Theory of Disuse" (Bjork & Bjork 1992) — storage strength vs. retrieval strength.
- **Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science*, 319(5865), 966–968.** Demonstrates the testing effect against students' own predictions — they thought re-reading would help more; retrieval helped more. The fluency illusion empirically.
- **Rawson, K. A., & Dunlosky, J. (2002). Are performance predictions for text based on ease of processing? *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 28(1), 69–80.** Text-fluency illusions.
- **Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. *Cognitive Science*, 12(2), 257–285.** Intrinsic vs. extraneous load (germane load added in later work, Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 1998).

### Key empirical cases
- Karpicke & Roediger 2008 testing effect demonstrations (foundational for the chapter's "fluency feels like learning but isn't" point).
- Maguire et al. (2000, 2006) London taxi driver hippocampal volume — for the Wayback Machine candidate.
- HM (Henry Molaison) work by Brenda Milner — historical anchor for memory neuroscience.

## 2. The Core Concept — State of the Field

### What is settled
- **Storage strength and retrieval strength dissociate.** Immediate performance reflects retrieval strength only; durable learning requires storage strength; the two are not the same variable. Bjork's two-factor model has held for fifty years.
- **The testing effect (retrieval practice).** Retrieval produces stronger storage than re-study. Replicated across content domains, ages, retention intervals. Meta-analyses report d ≈ 0.50–0.61 (Adesope, Trevisan, & Sundararajan 2017).
- **Desirable difficulties.** Spacing, interleaving, varying conditions, and retrieval practice all *depress* immediate performance and *elevate* delayed performance. Counter-intuitive to students and instructors; robust empirically.
- **Prediction errors drive associative learning.** TD-learning models fit dopamine neural data; behavioral learning rates correlate with prediction error magnitude.
- **Cognitive load is real and measurable.** Intrinsic load (inherent in material), extraneous load (instructional design), germane load (devoted to schema construction) — Sweller's tripartite structure remains the field consensus.

### What is disputed
- **Whether BDNF and dendritic spine work translate cleanly to human classroom learning.** The mechanistic chain is plausible; direct evidence in cognitively-loaded human learning is sparse.
- **Whether prediction error in cognitive learning is the *same* signal as prediction error in motor/reward learning.** Computational structure is shared; whether identical neurobiology applies is debated.
- **The boundary between desirable and undesirable difficulty.** Sweller's cognitive load community emphasizes load reduction; Bjork's community emphasizes productive difficulty. The frameworks coexist uneasily; the resolution is element-interactivity-dependent.
- **Whether "germane load" is a coherent construct** — Kalyuga (2011) and others argue it is not separately measurable from intrinsic load.

### What has changed recently (last 5 years)
- Larger meta-analyses of spacing and testing effects (Adesope 2017; Latimier et al. 2021) have firmed up effect sizes and identified moderators (delay, complexity, learner ability).
- Neuroimaging studies of retrieval practice (van den Broek et al. 2016) link the testing effect to lateral prefrontal and parietal activation; mechanism is *not* settled at the human neural level.
- Application of these findings to AI-assisted contexts is *new and thin* — Bastani 2025 is the strongest evidence that AI access damages durable retention even when immediate performance rises.

## 3. Application Domain Examples

1. **High school biology vocabulary.** Students re-read terms (massed, fluent) vs. self-test with flashcards (effortful, retrieval). Immediate quiz: re-reading slightly higher. Two-week quiz: retrieval substantially higher. Classic classroom demonstration; the reader will recognize it.
2. **Undergraduate calculus problem sets.** Worked-example study (low cognitive load, high immediate confidence) vs. interleaved problem solving (high cognitive load, low immediate confidence). The interleaved cohort scores lower on the next homework set, higher on the final exam.
3. **Nursing skills lab.** Same procedure practiced four times in one session vs. four times across four weeks. Immediate competency identical; six-week retention favors spaced.
4. **Medical clinical reasoning.** Students who construct differentials before seeing the case answer (productive struggle) outperform on novel cases. Students given the answer first show borrowed certainty's signature — fluent in this case, poor in the next.
5. **Middle school history essays.** Students who draft, revise, and abandon paragraphs (visible struggle in version history) outperform on the closed-book essay assessment over students whose draft history is short and linear.

## 4. The Book's Thesis Connection

Chapter 2 establishes the **mechanistic warrant** for the seven-signal architecture. The argument structure:

1. Genuine learning is a *neurobiological event* — prediction error → dopaminergic signal → synaptic modification → schema update.
2. Each neurobiological event has *behavioral consequences* — re-reading on confusion, error-correction trajectory, calibration update, decay pattern.
3. AI bypass *prevents* the neurobiological event from occurring. The student who reads the AI's answer experiences no prediction error (no mismatch with expectation), produces no dopaminergic learning signal, builds no schema update.
4. Therefore the behavioral traces *cannot be present* in the AI-bypass case, because the events that produce them did not occur.
5. The seven signals are not arbitrary — each is the behavioral signature of one or more of these mechanistic events:
   - Y1 (Temporal Engagement) — cognitive load distribution
   - Y2 (Error Trajectory) — prediction-error-driven schema update
   - Y3 (Cross-Context Transfer) — schema abstraction
   - Y4 (Uncertainty Calibration) — metacognitive monitoring
   - Y5 (Social Knowledge Texture) — specific encounter
   - Y6 (Retrieval Strength Decay) — storage-retrieval dissociation
   - Y7 (Scaffolding Response) — ZPD as developing schema

Chapter 2 is the bridge between Bastani-as-phenomenon (Ch 1) and the seven-component apparatus (Ch 3+).

**TIKTOC Part 10 explicit caution:** The chapter must present neurobiology as *the mechanism by which the traces exist*, not as *direct empirical evidence for GLP*. Tone rule: "this is why the mechanism works" not "this is the clinical evidence for the framework."

## 5. The AI Wayback Machine — Candidate Figures

**Candidate 1: Eleanor Maguire (b. 1970, Ireland).** UCL; the London taxi driver hippocampus studies (Maguire 2000 *PNAS*; Maguire 2006 *Hippocampus*). Knowledge of the Knowledge — the years of effortful spatial learning required to memorize London's streets — produced *measurable* posterior hippocampal volume increase. The most accessible, public-facing demonstration of structural brain change from sustained effortful learning. Satisfies: woman, alive, accessible, the perfect figure for "learning leaves physical traces." Sample prompt: "Eleanor, when you measured the taxi drivers' hippocampi, you were measuring the residue of years of effortful retrieval against difficulty. What would you measure if you wanted to see whether someone had actually learned, in a world where the artifact of learning can be produced without the effort?"

**Candidate 2: Brenda Milner (b. 1918, Canada/UK).** McGill; HM case studies; foundational figure in memory neuroscience. Woman, oldest living major neuroscientist as of 2026. Satisfies: woman, non-American (UK-born Canadian). Less directly relevant to the chapter's mechanism — Milner's work is about *what memory is*, not about *how learning leaves traces*. Use as secondary if Maguire is unavailable.

**Candidate 3: Wolfram Schultz (b. 1944, Germany).** Cambridge; the dopamine prediction-error finding (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). Non-American. Sample prompt: "Wolfram, your monkeys fired their dopamine neurons when the reward was unexpected. They stopped firing when the reward was predicted. What does that tell us about the student who reads the AI's answer before trying to solve the problem?" *But:* Schultz is famous in his field, and the chapter's primary reader will not have heard of him. Use if the chapter wants a name attached to the prediction-error mechanism.

## 6. Pedagogical Delivery Research

The chapter's hardest pedagogical move is **introducing neurobiology to a non-research-literate practitioner without losing her.** Three principles from the science-communication literature:

1. **Anchor every mechanism in an observable behavioral consequence.** Don't introduce dopamine and walk away. Introduce dopamine → "the moment of being wrong feels distinct because something at the cellular level is changing" → "the student who is never wrong because the AI was right for her never has that moment." Each mechanism must produce a behavior the reader has seen.
2. **The teacher-vocabulary translation.** "Prediction error" → "the surprise of being wrong." "Schema update" → "the moment something clicks into place." "Storage strength" → "what's still there in two weeks." "Retrieval strength" → "what's available right now." TIKTOC Part 9 tone rule: translated *before* used.
3. **The desirable-difficulties counter-intuitive structure.** Bjork & Bjork (2011) recommends *naming the counter-intuition first*: "students who feel they are learning faster usually aren't." The reader can then test the claim against her own observations of fluent-but-shallow students.

The Karpicke & Roediger 2008 study is the chapter's pedagogical workhorse. Students *predicted* re-reading would help more than testing. They were wrong. The instructor reader has seen this exact dynamic and will recognize the fluency illusion immediately.

## 7. Representation and Display Research

Chapter 2 is *not* a Y1–Y7 chapter, so the "How to observe without a platform" section is *not required*. But the chapter should preview the observability question — it should make the reader feel that traces are *real and observable*, even if the apparatus arrives in Ch 3.

Suggested figures:
- **The storage/retrieval two-axis diagram.** Performance on the y-axis, time on the x-axis. Two curves: high-immediate-low-durable (borrowed) and lower-immediate-higher-durable (genuine). The single most important picture in learning science for this audience.
- **The taxi driver hippocampus.** Maguire's image showing posterior hippocampal volume in taxi drivers vs. controls. The "learning leaves physical traces" visual.
- **The Karpicke-Roediger crossover.** Predicted performance (students think re-reading will help) vs. actual performance (testing wins). The fluency illusion in one chart.

Avoid: any neuroanatomy schematic that requires the reader to learn brain structures. Avoid: time-course graphs of dopamine firing. These are the kinds of figures that signal "this is a research book" and lose the practitioner.

## 8. Open Questions and Research Gaps

- **How much neurobiological detail is right?** TIKTOC Open Question 1. The preprint goes deep. The practitioner book should establish *that* learning is a neurobiological event and *that* the events produce behavioral traces — then move on. Suggested: one paragraph each on prediction error, BDNF, and spines, no more.
- **Whether the BDNF/spines claims hold up to scrutiny from a neuroscience-literate reader.** The framing must be airtight: "these are the mechanisms by which learning produces durable change" — not "these are the events that occur when a student writes an essay." Get a neuroscientist reviewer.
- **Is there a single accessible study that puts cognitive learning, storage strength, and decay together in humans?** Karpicke & Roediger 2008 is closest; better candidates may exist.
- **The fluency trap empirically.** Most of the fluency-illusion literature is about text re-reading. Is there literature specifically on *AI-generated-explanation* fluency illusions? Likely thin or absent — flag as opening for the framework.
